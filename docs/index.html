The AI industry

Google loses $500bn in value as its own AI search undercuts ad sales

Alphabet's shift towards AI-generated search answers appears to be eating into the advertising revenue that has funded the company for two decades.

Alphabet, Google's parent company, saw roughly $500 billion wiped off its market value this week, according to the BBC, as investors reacted to signs that its AI-powered search features are cutting into advertising income.

Google has spent the past two years rolling out AI Overviews and other generative-search tools that answer questions directly on the results page, rather than sending users to click through to websites. That's good for users wanting fast answers, but it reduces the number of ad clicks that have historically made Google enormous profits.

The episode captures a broader anxiety running through the AI industry this autumn: the technology companies are spending at a record pace to build AI systems, but it's not yet clear whether those systems will grow revenue or quietly erode the businesses that are paying for them.

Sources: BBC

Key concepts in this story
Market capitalisation
This is the total value of a company's shares on the stock market, calculated by multiplying the share price by the number of shares. A $500 billion drop means investors collectively decided the company is worth $500 billion less than before.
Generative search
Instead of showing a list of links, an AI-powered search engine writes a direct answer by summarising information, a bit like asking a well-read friend instead of being handed a stack of books.
Why it matters for you

If AI search genuinely erodes ad revenue, it strengthens the case that today's AI boom is partly self-cannibalising, a useful nuance to mention in any interview question about AI business models.

The AI industry

Intel shares crash 28%, cutting $12bn from the US government's stake

A weak sales outlook sent Intel's stock tumbling, denting the value of the government's year-old 10% holding in the chipmaker.

Intel's stock fell around 28% on Friday after the company forecast fourth-quarter revenue of $15–16 billion, well below the $17 billion analysts expected, and warned that profit margins would shrink sharply. The slump reportedly cut $12 billion from the value of the US government's 10% stake in the company, taken under the Trump administration last year.

Chief executive Lip-Bu Tan blamed a slowdown in PC sales, which he linked directly to the AI boom: soaring demand for memory chips used in AI data centres has pushed up prices and availability problems for the components that go into ordinary laptops and desktops, squeezing IT budgets. Intel's own data centre business, by contrast, grew 78% year-on-year, though the firm can't fully cash in until new chip factories, or fabs, come online — one in Arizona is already running, but the next isn't expected until 2028.

Despite Friday's rout, the government's stake, along with Nvidia's own investment in Intel made earlier this year, is still up more than 70% in value since it was bought. The episode shows how the AI build-out is reshaping even unrelated corners of the tech industry, as component shortages ripple outward from data centres into everyday consumer electronics.

Sources: TechCrunch

Key concepts in this story
Gross margin
This is the percentage of revenue left after subtracting the direct cost of making a product. Intel expects its margin to fall from 43% to 37%, meaning it keeps less profit from every dollar of sales.
Semiconductor fab
A fab, short for fabrication plant, is the hugely expensive factory where silicon chips are physically manufactured. Building a new one can cost tens of billions of dollars and take years.
Equity stake valuation gain/loss
When a government or company owns shares in another firm, the value of that stake rises and falls with the share price, even if nothing is bought or sold.
Why it matters for you

Component shortages caused by AI demand are a real-world example of how a tech trend in one sector (data centres) can quietly raise costs in another (PCs) — a good talking point for understanding supply chain risk in tech investing.

The AI industry

Big Tech's $3 trillion AI bet starts to strain its cash machines

Meta, Amazon and Alphabet all posted negative free cash flow this quarter as capital spending on AI infrastructure accelerates, leaving Microsoft as the only hyperscaler still generating cash.

The world's largest tech companies collectively hold around $3 trillion in AI-related spending commitments, according to Bloomberg, and this earnings season showed the strain that is starting to put on their day-to-day finances. Meta posted negative free cash flow of $2.12 billion, Amazon's outflow swelled to $42.4 billion, and Alphabet's reached $9.07 billion. Microsoft's own cash generation fell 27% to $18.63 billion and the company said it would now borrow money in the bond market to keep funding AI infrastructure.

These companies are relying on profitable 'legacy' businesses — search ads, cloud computing, online retail, business software — to bankroll AI data centres that won't pay off for years, if at all. Amazon said its retail and advertising growth slowed, with online sales up just 2% year-on-year compared with 10% a year earlier, while its capital expenditure, the money spent on physical assets like servers and buildings, is expected to hit $220 billion this year and climb further in 2027. Alphabet's results worried investors most, with its search and YouTube advertising growth slowing for a third straight quarter amid competition from AI chatbots.

Analysts are split on how worried to be. Some point out that Alphabet's $40 billion-plus quarterly operating income dwarfs its cash flow gap, unlike loss-making AI labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic. Others warn that if AI spending keeps growing faster than the legacy businesses that fund it, today's comfortable cushion could erode. None of the hyperscalers gave firm capex targets for next year, but all signalled the spending spree is set to continue.

Sources: Tech · Bloomberg

Key concepts in this story
Free cash flow
This is the cash a company has left after paying for its operations and investments. Negative free cash flow means a company is spending more cash than it brings in, and typically needs savings or borrowing to cover the gap.
Capital expenditure (capex)
Capex is money spent on long-term physical assets, like data centres or chips, as opposed to day-to-day running costs. If a company spends $220 billion on capex but only earns $200 billion from operations, it must borrow or sell assets to cover the difference.
Operating income
This is profit from a company's core business operations before interest and tax are taken out. Alphabet's $40 billion-plus quarterly operating income shows its underlying business is still highly profitable, even while cash flow looks weak.
Why it matters for you

Understanding the gap between a company's reported profit and its actual cash flow is a core skill for finance and equity research roles, and it's exactly the kind of number an interviewer might ask you to interpret.

Nasdaq loses around $2 trillion in October as AI spending fears grow

A month that began with AI-driven optimism ended with a steep tech sell-off as investors questioned whether spending will ever pay off.

The Nasdaq index reportedly shed around $2 trillion in value during October, with roughly $1 trillion of that wiped out in a single stretch of trading, according to Reuters and NBC News. The index fell more than 700 points in one session as investors pulled back from major technology names.

The slide came as doubts mounted over whether the enormous sums tech giants are pouring into AI infrastructure will generate matching returns, following a string of earnings reports showing strained cash flow at companies like Amazon, Meta and Alphabet.

The sell-off underscores how closely linked the stock market has become to a handful of AI-exposed companies, meaning any wobble in confidence about AI spending can ripple through retirement funds and index trackers well beyond Silicon Valley.

Sources: Reuters · NBC News · CBS News

Key concepts in this story
Market correction
A correction is usually defined as a drop of 10% or more in a stock index from its recent peak, reflecting a sudden shift in investor sentiment rather than necessarily a change in company fundamentals.
Nasdaq Composite
This is a stock market index tracking thousands of companies, heavily weighted towards technology firms, making it a useful barometer for how investors feel about the tech and AI sector specifically.
Why it matters for you

A sharp market move like this is a classic interview talking point for understanding sentiment-driven risk, separate from the underlying business fundamentals covered in today's other stories.

AI's circular financing web spreads into Asia

Deals in which AI firms fund their own customers and suppliers are reportedly expanding into Asian markets, raising fears of wider financial contagion.

Bloomberg reports that the pattern of circular financing seen among major US AI players — where a company invests in or lends to a customer that then uses the money to buy its products — is spreading into Asia, widening concerns about contagion risk across global markets.

Circular financing arrangements have already drawn scrutiny in the US AI boom because they can make revenue and demand look stronger than they would be through ordinary market transactions, since money is effectively moving in a loop between a small number of related companies.

With few further details confirmed, the story adds to a growing list of concerns this week about how interconnected AI-related financing has become, and how a problem at one firm could spread more widely than expected.

Sources: Bloomberg

Key concepts in this story
Circular financing
This happens when Company A invests in Company B, and Company B then uses that money to buy products or services from Company A, making both companies' numbers look better than they would from independent trade.
Contagion risk
This is the danger that financial trouble at one company or market spreads to others it's connected to, similar to how one person's illness can spread through a crowded room if everyone is in close contact.
Why it matters for you

Spotting circular financing is an increasingly important skill in equity analysis and credit risk roles, since it can hide how dependent a company's growth really is on a few related deals.

AI & finance

Canadian banks block personal AI agents over security fears

BMO, CIBC, RBC and TD have reportedly cut off access for AI agents trying to act on customers' behalf, as US open banking rules remain stalled.

Several of Canada's largest banks — BMO, CIBC, RBC and TD — have blocked personal AI agents from accessing customer accounts, citing safety and security concerns, according to Radio-Canada and AI News. The move affects AI tools designed to carry out tasks like payments or account management automatically on a user's behalf.

The block comes as progress on an open banking rule in the US, which would require banks to let customers securely share their financial data with third-party apps, has reportedly stalled. Without clear rules, banks are wary of letting autonomous software touch sensitive financial systems where mistakes or exploits could cause real financial harm.

The episode is a reminder that even as AI agents are hyped across the tech industry, regulated sectors like banking are moving cautiously, prioritising security and regulatory clarity over speed of adoption.

Sources: AI News · Radio-Canada · ad-hoc-news.de

Key concepts in this story
AI agent
An AI agent is a system that can take multi-step actions on a user's behalf, such as logging into an account and making a payment, rather than just answering questions like a chatbot.
Open Banking
Open banking is a framework that lets customers securely share their financial data with approved third-party apps and services, usually via standardised technical connections called APIs, rather than banks keeping all the data locked away.
Why it matters for you

Financial institutions blocking AI agents highlights a real tension between the hype around agentic AI and the practical risk management needed in regulated industries, which is exactly the kind of judgement call fintech and compliance roles are built around.

Startups & funding

AI inference startup Baseten eyes $30bn valuation

Baseten, which helps companies run AI models in production, is reportedly closing a new funding round that would value it at $30 billion.

Baseten, a startup that specialises in running AI models for other companies, is reportedly set for a $30 billion valuation following a new funding round, according to Bloomberg and Tech in Asia. The company's business focuses on inference, the process of actually running a trained AI model to generate answers for real users, as opposed to the earlier, more expensive process of training the model in the first place.

Demand for inference infrastructure has surged as more companies move AI products from demos into everyday use, since every chatbot reply or AI-generated image requires computing power at the moment a user asks for it. Specialist inference providers like Baseten position themselves as an alternative to running that workload directly on a hyperscaler's cloud.

Limited details are confirmed beyond the headline figures, but the scale of the valuation underlines investor appetite for the 'plumbing' layer of the AI industry, not just the flashy foundation models themselves.

Sources: Bloomberg · Tech in Asia

Key concepts in this story
Inference
Inference is when a trained AI model is actually used to produce an answer, such as generating a reply to your question. Training a model might take weeks and huge computing resources, but inference happens every single time someone uses it, often in under a second.
Valuation
A company's valuation is what investors agree it is worth, usually based on a funding round's share price multiplied by total shares. A $30 billion valuation means investors believe the company could eventually be worth that much, not that it currently has that much cash or profit.
Why it matters for you

Big valuations for inference-focused startups show that there's serious money to be made in the unglamorous infrastructure behind AI products, which is useful context if you're considering a career in machine learning engineering or MLOps rather than model research.

Startups & funding

Apple acquires agentic AI data startup Ascend.io

Apple has reportedly bought Ascend.io, a startup building agentic tools for automating data engineering work.

Apple has reportedly acquired Ascend.io, a startup that builds agentic AI tools aimed at automating data engineering, the unglamorous but essential work of collecting, cleaning and organising data so it can be used elsewhere, according to multiple outlets including 9to5Mac and Cult of Mac. Terms of the deal have not been disclosed.

Ascend.io's pitch has reportedly been to make data engineering 'delightful' by using AI agents to handle repetitive data pipeline tasks automatically, rather than requiring engineers to write and maintain that code by hand. For Apple, which has lagged some rivals in visible generative AI products, the acquisition suggests continued investment in the less visible infrastructure needed to build and improve AI features across its products.

Details beyond the headline acquisition remain limited, but the deal fits a wider pattern this year of large tech companies buying smaller AI startups to absorb both their technology and their engineering teams rather than building equivalent tools from scratch.

Sources: Cult of Mac · 9to5Mac · MacDailyNews

Key concepts in this story
Agentic AI
Agentic AI refers to systems that can independently carry out multi-step tasks, like organising a messy dataset end-to-end, rather than simply responding to a single prompt.
Data engineering
Data engineering is the work of building systems that collect, clean and move data so it's ready for use by analysts or AI models, similar to laying plumbing before water can flow through a building.
Acquisition
An acquisition is when one company buys another, usually to gain its technology, products or staff rather than building those things internally from zero.
Why it matters for you

Acquisitions like this show that even giants like Apple prefer buying proven AI talent and tools over building everything in-house, which is worth knowing if you're weighing up joining an AI startup versus a big tech company.

Code corner

Simulating an LLM inference API call

Based on: AI inference startup Baseten eyes $30bn valuation

Baseten's business is all about inference: running an already-trained AI model to answer real user requests. Here's a simplified, offline example of how a piece of software might call an LLM API and work out roughly what that request cost.

import json

def fake_llm_api_call(prompt):
    # Pretend this is a response from an LLM provider's API
    fake_response = {
        "model": "demo-llm-1",
        "output_text": "Inference is when a trained model generates a response.",
        "usage": {"prompt_tokens": 12, "completion_tokens": 10}
    }
    return json.dumps(fake_response)

def estimate_cost(usage, price_per_1k_tokens=0.002):
    total_tokens = usage["prompt_tokens"] + usage["completion_tokens"]
    cost = (total_tokens / 1000) * price_per_1k_tokens
    return total_tokens, cost

prompt = "Explain inference in one sentence."
raw_response = fake_llm_api_call(prompt)
response = json.loads(raw_response)

tokens, cost = estimate_cost(response["usage"])

print("Model reply:", response["output_text"])
print("Tokens used:", tokens)
print(f"Estimated cost: ${cost:.5f}")
Line by line
  1. `fake_llm_api_call` stands in for a real API request, returning a JSON string just like a real LLM provider would, including token usage.
  2. `json.dumps` turns a Python dictionary into a JSON-formatted string, mimicking what an API actually sends over the network.
  3. `json.loads` does the reverse, converting that JSON string back into a Python dictionary we can work with.
  4. `estimate_cost` adds up prompt and completion tokens (the units LLMs are billed by) and multiplies by a per-1,000-token price to estimate the cost of one inference call.
  5. The final print statements show the model's reply, how many tokens it used, and roughly what that single request would cost a company like Baseten's customers.

Try it: Change the `price_per_1k_tokens` value to $0.01 and re-run the code to see how much the estimated cost increases, then try adding a longer fake `output_text` and increasing `completion_tokens` to see the effect on cost.
