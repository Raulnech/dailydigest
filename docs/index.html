Nvidia Hires Wachtell Lipton as Huang Affair Probe Shadows Earnings Week

Jensen Huang returned to his desk this week projecting calm, but his board has launched an independent investigation into an affair reportedly linked to Chinese intelligence.

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang held his usual morning executive meeting on Monday, just a day after telling staff he'd had an extramarital affair and a week before the company reports quarterly earnings. He apologised to employees but took no questions before moving on to business as usual, according to people familiar with the meeting.

The affair has taken on much bigger stakes because the woman involved, a former Megaspeed executive, has reportedly been linked by the New York Times and Financial Times to Chinese intelligence. Nvidia's board has opened an investigation and hired law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to lead it, with lead independent director Stephen Neal, whose past clients include Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, expected to play a central role.

Huang, who co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and has become the public face of the AI boom, is also a key figure in US-China trade diplomacy, having travelled with President Trump to China and dined alongside President Xi at a White House state dinner. The scandal has intensified scrutiny of his lobbying for continued chip sales to China, right as Nvidia's quarterly results and broader confidence in the AI buildout hang in the balance.

Sources: Tech · Tech · Tech

Key concepts in this story
Due diligence
This means carefully checking the facts and risks around a person or deal before acting, like a buyer inspecting a house before signing contracts. Nvidia's board is doing due diligence on Huang's conduct and any security implications.
Conflict of interest
This is when someone's personal situation could compromise their professional judgement, such as a CEO's relationship affecting decisions on sensitive export policy.
Reputational risk
This is the danger that bad publicity damages trust in a company, even if no law was broken. Investors worry reputational risk at Nvidia could spill over into the whole AI sector's share prices.
Why it matters for you

Corporate governance and crisis-management skills are increasingly valued alongside technical AI knowledge; in interviews you can note how a CEO scandal at a company as systemically important as Nvidia creates ripple effects across markets and policy, a useful example of why risk teams matter.

The AI industry

Google Launches Two New TPU Chips to Challenge Nvidia

Google's eighth-generation TPU family grows with chips built for secure AI training and for handling much larger workloads.

Google unveiled two new tensor processing units (TPUs) on Monday: the TPU 8tx, designed with AMD for confidential AI training, and the TPU 8i Max, which doubles the memory and processing power of its existing inference chip by combining two units into one package. Both chips expand Google's in-house silicon lineup beyond its earlier eighth-generation TPUs launched in April.

The TPU 8tx is built so that sensitive data and model weights stay protected even while a model is actively running, making it attractive to companies that want to train AI on proprietary data without moving that data elsewhere. The TPU 8i Max, meanwhile, lets AI models process far larger amounts of information per query thanks to its expanded memory capacity.

The launch fits a broader pattern of hyperscalers and AI labs such as Microsoft, Amazon, OpenAI and Anthropic building their own chips to reduce reliance on Nvidia. The timing is notable, landing just as Nvidia faces distraction from the fallout of CEO Jensen Huang's affair disclosure, adding to questions about whether rivals can chip away at Nvidia's dominant position.

Sources: TechCrunch · Yahoo! Finance Canada

Key concepts in this story
ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit)
This is a chip custom-built for one specific job rather than general computing, making it faster and more efficient at that task. Google's TPUs are ASICs designed specifically for AI workloads, unlike general-purpose CPUs.
Confidential computing
This is a way of processing data so it stays encrypted and protected even while it is being actively used, not just when stored or sent. It matters for companies that don't want their proprietary data exposed during AI training.
Memory bandwidth
This is how much data a chip can move in and out of memory per second, similar to how wide a motorway determines how much traffic can flow through. More memory bandwidth lets an AI model handle bigger chunks of information at once.
Why it matters for you

Understanding why companies design their own chips instead of buying Nvidia's helps explain cost and margin pressures across the AI industry, a useful talking point if you're asked in an interview why cloud providers are investing billions in custom silicon.

Blacklisted Chinese University Bought Nvidia H200 Chips, Records Show

Procurement records reportedly show Northwestern Polytechnical University, which has ties to China's military and has been on a US export blacklist for 25 years, purchased Nvidia's H200 AI chips last month.

Procurement notices reviewed by Reuters reportedly show that Northwestern Polytechnical University (NWPU), a Chinese institution with links to the People's Liberation Army, bought at least four Nvidia H200 chips despite being on the US Entity List, a blacklist barring American firms from exporting sensitive technology to it without a licence.

NWPU has been restricted by US export controls since 2001, long before the current wave of AI chip restrictions began. The H200 is one of Nvidia's higher-end AI chips, and its appearance at a blacklisted military-linked university raises questions about how export controls are being enforced and whether banned buyers are obtaining chips through intermediaries.

The report adds to ongoing scrutiny of Nvidia's China sales just as its CEO faces separate controversy over an alleged Chinese intelligence connection, intensifying political pressure in Washington over whether current export rules are tough enough.

Sources: Reuters · NDTV · WION

Key concepts in this story
Entity List
This is a US government blacklist of organisations barred from receiving certain American technology without special permission, similar to a no-sale list. Being on it since 2001 means NWPU should legally be unable to buy advanced Nvidia chips.
Export control evasion
This refers to blocked buyers getting restricted goods anyway, often by routing purchases through third parties or shell companies. If confirmed, this case would be an example of such evasion involving AI chips.
Why it matters for you

Export control enforcement is a growing compliance and policy field, and this story is a good example to cite if asked how national security concerns intersect with AI hardware supply chains in a finance or policy interview.

Morgan Stanley Says AI Debt Deals Now Face a 'Higher Bar'

The bank is reportedly warning that lenders are growing more cautious about financing riskier AI infrastructure debt.

Morgan Stanley has reportedly signalled that riskier debt deals tied to AI infrastructure now face tougher scrutiny from lenders, as concerns mount over how sustainable the current pace of AI-related borrowing really is.

The warning reflects a broader shift in how banks and credit investors are approaching the huge sums being borrowed to fund data centres and chip purchases, after a period in which capital flowed relatively easily into the sector.

Sources: Bloomberg.com

Key concepts in this story
Underwriting standards
These are the rules lenders use to decide who qualifies for a loan and on what terms, like a landlord checking a tenant's income before renting a flat. Tighter underwriting standards mean fewer risky AI deals get financed, or only at a higher cost.
Risk premium
This is the extra return investors demand for taking on a riskier investment, on top of a safe baseline rate. A 'higher bar' for AI debt deals usually means lenders are charging a bigger risk premium.
Why it matters for you

If you want to work in credit or leveraged finance, understanding why banks tighten lending standards for a hot sector like AI is exactly the kind of judgement interviewers probe for.

AI Could Cut Banks' Costs by 20%, But Most Haven't Seen It Yet

McKinsey finds AI has boosted individual bank workers' productivity but has barely moved the industry's overall bottom line, while AI agent costs are proving harder to control than expected.

A new McKinsey report finds that artificial intelligence could eventually cut the banking industry's cost base by 20% or more, but says most banks have so far only used the technology to make individual employees more productive, with negligible effect on overall profits.

Separately, McKinsey notes that banks are now trying to rein in the costs of running AI agents, software systems that can autonomously complete multi-step tasks, after finding these systems have become more expensive to operate at scale than initially projected.

The findings suggest a gap between AI's promised efficiency gains and what banks are actually capturing, raising questions about whether the sector is investing wisely or simply chasing a trend without a clear path to profit.

Sources: Finextra Research Headlines · Reuters

Key concepts in this story
Cost base
This is the total amount a business spends to run itself, including staff, technology and operations. Cutting the cost base by 20% would mean a bank spends a fifth less to do the same amount of business.
Return on investment (ROI)
This measures whether the money and effort put into something, like an AI project, actually pays off in savings or profit. McKinsey's findings suggest many banks have spent on AI without yet seeing a clear ROI.
Why it matters for you

This is a direct insight into why banks are hiring data and AI specialists right now, but also why they're becoming pickier about which AI projects get funded, a nuance worth raising if asked about AI adoption in a banking interview.

JPMorgan Expands Australia Corporate Bank Team to Chase AI and Mining Deals

The bank is reportedly growing its corporate banking presence in Australia to capture dealmaking tied to AI infrastructure and the mining sector.

JPMorgan is reportedly expanding its corporate banking team in Australia, aiming to capture more business from companies involved in AI infrastructure and mining, two sectors seeing significant investment activity.

The move suggests banks see Australia's AI data centre buildout and resource-rich mining industry as a growing source of deal fees, even as other parts of the AI financing world face more caution.

Sources: Bloomberg.com

Key concepts in this story
Corporate bank
This is the part of a bank that provides services like loans, advice and deal-making to companies rather than individual consumers. Growing a corporate bank team usually signals a bank expects more big-company deals in that market.
Deal flow
This refers to the stream of potential transactions, like mergers or financings, that a bank or investor gets to see and potentially work on. More deal flow in AI and mining means more fee opportunities for JPMorgan's bankers.
Why it matters for you

Regional hiring moves like this are a signal of where banks expect deal activity to grow, useful intelligence if you're targeting a career in corporate banking or deal advisory.

AI Training Startup Mercor Doubles Valuation to $20 Billion

Mercor, which supplies human-labelled data to train AI models, raised $750 million in a new funding round that doubled its valuation in a year.

Mercor, a startup that provides AI labs with human experts to label and evaluate training data, has raised $750 million in a Series D funding round that values the company at $20 billion, roughly double its valuation from a year earlier.

The rapid rise reflects how valuable data labelling and evaluation work has become as AI labs compete to improve model quality, since large language models need carefully checked, high-quality examples to learn from and to be tested against.

The scale of the round, and the speed of Mercor's valuation growth, underscores investor appetite for companies positioned as essential infrastructure to the AI boom, even as questions persist elsewhere about whether AI valuations broadly are overheating.

Sources: Reuters · MLQ.ai · Traders Union

Key concepts in this story
Series D funding
This is a later-stage round of venture capital funding, typically for companies that have already proven their business model and are scaling up, as opposed to earlier seed or Series A rounds. Reaching Series D with a $20 billion valuation shows strong investor confidence in Mercor's growth.
Data labelling
This is the process of humans tagging or checking information, like marking whether an AI's answer was correct, so that a model can learn from it. High-quality data labelling is one reason some AI models perform noticeably better than others.
Why it matters for you

Data labelling and evaluation work is becoming a genuine career path in AI, and knowing that human feedback still underpins model quality is a useful point to make if you're asked how LLMs actually get better over time.

Oura Buys AI Doctor Startup Counsel Health for $625 Million

The smart ring maker is turning to acquisitions after shelving its IPO, snapping up an AI-powered primary care platform as its revenue jumps 153%.

Oura, the smart ring company, announced on Tuesday it is acquiring Counsel Health, an AI-powered primary care platform, for $625 million. The deal comes weeks after Oura postponed its planned initial public offering, which could have raised up to $2.2 billion, citing uncertainty in the IPO market.

Counsel Health combines a chat-based AI medical assistant with access to licensed physicians, and has already been integrated into the Oura app since June. The expanded partnership aims to cover preventative, acute, specialty and chronic care, with Counsel's CEO joining Oura as chief clinical AI officer alongside the startup's 70 employees.

The acquisition comes as Oura faces growing competition from rivals like RingConn and Ultrahuman in the smart ring market, and as the company posted strong results: revenue grew 153% year-on-year to $533 million last quarter, with full-year revenue reaching around $1.75 billion. Oura was valued at $11 billion after a funding round last October, making this deal a sign that companies with stalled IPO plans are instead using cash and strong growth to buy their way into new markets.

Sources: TechCrunch · Bloomberg.com · Quartz

Key concepts in this story
Series A funding
This is typically the first major round of venture capital a startup raises after proving an initial idea works, usually used to build out the product and team. Counsel Health had raised a $25 million Series A a year before being acquired.
Revenue growth rate
This measures how much a company's sales have increased over a period, usually shown as a percentage. Oura's 153% revenue growth means its sales more than doubled compared with the same quarter last year.
Why it matters for you

This story shows how companies are substituting acquisitions for IPOs when public markets feel shaky, a pattern worth recognising if you're tracking exits in AI health tech or considering a career in corporate development.

Code corner

Costing Out an AI Agent's API Calls

Based on: AI Could Cut Banks' Costs by 20%, But Most Haven't Seen It Yet

McKinsey's report said banks are struggling to control the cost of running AI agents. Here's a simple way to model that: simulate a fake LLM API response and calculate how much a batch of agent calls would cost based on tokens used.

# Fake LLM API response, like you'd get back from a real provider
fake_response = {
    "id": "chatcmpl-123",
    "model": "gpt-mini",
    "usage": {
        "prompt_tokens": 120,
        "completion_tokens": 80
    },
    "choices": [
        {"message": {"role": "assistant", "content": "Loan approved pending review."}}
    ]
}

# Pretend pricing: cost per 1000 tokens
PRICE_PROMPT_PER_1K = 0.003
PRICE_COMPLETION_PER_1K = 0.006

def calculate_cost(usage):
    prompt_cost = (usage["prompt_tokens"] / 1000) * PRICE_PROMPT_PER_1K
    completion_cost = (usage["completion_tokens"] / 1000) * PRICE_COMPLETION_PER_1K
    return prompt_cost + completion_cost

cost_per_call = calculate_cost(fake_response["usage"])
print(f"Cost per call: ${cost_per_call:.5f}")

# Now scale it up: a bank's AI agent handles 50,000 calls a day
daily_calls = 50000
daily_cost = cost_per_call * daily_calls
print(f"Estimated daily cost: ${daily_cost:,.2f}")
print(f"Estimated annual cost: ${daily_cost * 365:,.2f}")
Line by line
  1. fake_response is a dictionary standing in for what a real LLM API would send back, including a 'usage' field showing how many tokens were used.
  2. Tokens are the small chunks of text (roughly parts of words) that LLMs process; both input ('prompt') and output ('completion') tokens usually cost money.
  3. calculate_cost() divides tokens by 1000 and multiplies by the per-1000-token price, since API pricing is usually quoted per 1000 tokens.
  4. We then scale a single call's cost up to 50,000 daily calls, showing how small per-call costs can add up to a very large annual bill, exactly the kind of surprise McKinsey says banks are now facing.

Try it: Change daily_calls to 500,000 to simulate a much bigger bank, and add a new agent type with a higher completion_tokens value (say 300) to see how answer length affects total cost.
