The AI industry

OpenAI signs four Abu Dhabi deals covering oil, government and investment

Sam Altman meets Abu Dhabi's crown prince as OpenAI deepens Gulf ties while chasing a fresh $20 billion funding round.

OpenAI has signed four agreements in Abu Dhabi spanning energy, government and investment, the Abu Dhabi Media Office said on Monday, as chief executive Sam Altman met Khaled bin Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, the emirate's crown prince. State oil company ADNOC, working with AI firm G42, agreed to deploy OpenAI's most advanced ('frontier') models and AI agents across its oil and gas operations. Sovereign investor Mubadala and Inception42 separately agreed to apply AI to investment analysis, while the Abu Dhabi government struck a deal to translate policies and regulations into machine-readable form.

The Gulf has become one of the most important regions for AI expansion, with US tech giants building huge data centres there even as regional conflicts test that appeal. OpenAI and rival Anthropic have increasingly turned to Middle Eastern investors for capital as the cost of training frontier AI models balloons. Abu Dhabi's MGX fund has backed both labs, co-leading Anthropic's $30 billion raise in February and OpenAI's $122 billion raise in March.

The timing matters: OpenAI is reportedly seeking to raise a further $20 billion, co-led by UAE investors, according to CNBC. Altman said the UAE was 'setting a bold, global standard' for AI adoption, framing the deals around safety and accessibility.

The agreements tighten the financial and operational links between a leading AI lab and Gulf state capital, raising questions about how deeply AI infrastructure becomes entwined with oil money, sovereign funds and government decision-making.

Sources: Tech · Investing.com UK · Crude Oil Prices Today | OilPrice.com

Key concepts in this story
Sovereign wealth fund
A reminder: this is a state-owned investment fund, often built from oil or gas revenue, that buys stakes in companies abroad. Abu Dhabi's Mubadala and MGX are examples investing heavily in AI.
AI agent
A reminder: an AI agent is software that can take multi-step actions on its own, such as monitoring an oil pump's sensors and deciding when to schedule maintenance, rather than just answering a single question.
Frontier AI lab
A reminder: this refers to companies like OpenAI and Anthropic building the most advanced, expensive AI models, as opposed to firms that just use existing models.
Why it matters for you

Deals like this create demand for people who understand both AI deployment and energy or government operations, so experience translating technical AI capability into industry-specific use cases is valuable. In interviews, you can mention how Gulf sovereign capital is becoming central to funding frontier AI labs.

The AI industry

Anthropic says AI now leads 26% of its own research, up from 1% in March

An internal Anthropic chart shows AI-led work on its next-generation Claude models has surged in eight months.

Anthropic has reportedly disclosed that AI systems now lead around 26% of the research and development work behind its next-generation Claude models, up from just 1% in March, according to an internal analysis cited by Dealroom and BigGo Finance.

The figure, described as one of the most closely watched charts among AI insiders, suggests AI labs are rapidly using their own models to speed up the process of building newer, better models - a trend sometimes called 'automating AI research itself'.

If accurate, this points to AI development accelerating faster than headcount alone could explain, since AI tools can write and test code or analyse experiment results without a person doing every step.

Sources: Dealroom · BigGo Finance

Key concepts in this story
Automation
A reminder: automation means a machine or program doing a task that previously needed a human, such as AI drafting and testing code changes for a new model version.
AI agent
A reminder: software that performs multi-step tasks with some autonomy, here apparently used to help design and test new versions of Claude.
Why it matters for you

This is a strong talking point for interviews about AI research productivity: it shows how AI companies are using their own models as tools, not just products, which is reshaping how fast the whole field moves.

The AI industry

Google and Anthropic push back on Japan's AI copyright transparency rules

Both companies reportedly say Japan's new AI transparency code limits how far regulators can probe their use of copyrighted material.

Google and Anthropic have reportedly told Japanese authorities that there are limits on how much they can disclose about copyright issues under Japan's AI transparency code, according to MLex.

The code is meant to make AI companies more open about what data they use to train models, amid growing global pressure from authors, publishers and artists who argue their copyrighted work is being used without permission or payment.

The pushback highlights an unresolved tension at the heart of AI regulation: governments want visibility into training data, while AI firms often argue that full disclosure is impractical or commercially sensitive.

Sources: MLex

Key concepts in this story
Regulator
A reminder: a regulator is a government body that sets and enforces rules for an industry, in this case overseeing how AI companies disclose the data used to train their models.
Copyright
Copyright is the legal right that gives creators control over use of their original work, such as books or articles. AI firms are being questioned over whether training models on copyrighted text without permission breaks this right.
Why it matters for you

Understanding AI copyright disputes is useful in both legal and product roles, as it shapes what data companies can legally use for training and could affect costs and licensing deals across the industry.

AI & finance

Marvell and Lumentum agree to combine in AI networking tie-up

The chipmaker and optical components maker plan to merge into what they call a category leader in AI connectivity, sending Marvell shares higher.

Marvell Technology and Lumentum have agreed to combine their businesses, aiming to create a leading supplier of the networking hardware that connects AI data centres together, according to Yahoo Finance. Marvell's stock rallied on the news, per Investing.com.

The deal brings together Marvell's chip expertise with Lumentum's optical components, which are used to move data at high speed between the thousands of GPUs that power AI training and inference. As AI data centres scale up, the links between chips become as important as the chips themselves.

The tie-up is part of a broader wave of consolidation in AI infrastructure supply chains, as companies race to control more of the 'full stack' needed to build and run AI systems at scale.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · Investing.com

Key concepts in this story
Acquisition
A reminder: this is when one company buys or merges with another, here combining chip-making with optical networking to serve AI data centres.
Full AI stack
A reminder: this means owning multiple layers needed to deliver AI, from chips and networking to software, rather than relying on outside suppliers for each piece.
Why it matters for you

This deal shows investors rewarding companies that control critical AI infrastructure bottlenecks like data centre networking, a trend worth mentioning if asked about where AI value is concentrating beyond chipmakers like Nvidia.

AI & finance

Anthropic reportedly seeks $3.5 billion from Japan amid tech sell-off

Anthropic is said to be raising fresh cash in Japan to fund AI computing power as tech stocks wobble and AI financing comes under strain.

Anthropic is reportedly seeking to raise $3.5 billion in Japan, according to Moomoo, as pressure mounts on AI financing amid a sharp sell-off in technology stocks.

The AI lab needs cash urgently to expand its computing capacity, the report says, reflecting how quickly the costs of running and training large AI models are rising even for well-funded firms.

The move comes as markets grow more cautious about heavy AI spending, making this an early signal of how AI labs might respond if investor appetite for funding rounds cools further.

Sources: Moomoo

Key concepts in this story
Market correction
A reminder: a market correction is a notable fall in asset prices, often 10% or more, following a period of strong gains. A tech sell-off can make it harder and pricier for companies to raise money.
Funding round
A reminder: this is when a company raises money from investors in exchange for equity or debt, used here by Anthropic to pay for expensive AI computing power.
Why it matters for you

If AI labs are raising cash during a tech sell-off, it signals growing pressure on the sector's financing model, which is directly relevant if you work in markets, venture capital or corporate finance roles touching AI.

AI & finance

S&P cuts Meta's outlook to negative over capex and cash flow risks

The ratings agency warns Meta's soaring AI spending could strain its finances, even as its core business remains highly profitable.

S&P Global has cut its outlook on Meta Platforms to negative, citing concerns over the company's elevated capital expenditure and the risks this poses to its cash flow, according to TradingView.

Meta has been pouring billions into AI infrastructure, including data centres and chips, as it competes with rivals like Google and Microsoft to build and run ever-larger AI models.

A negative outlook does not mean an immediate downgrade, but it signals that S&P sees a real chance Meta's credit rating could be cut if spending keeps outpacing the cash the business generates.

Sources: TradingView

Key concepts in this story
Credit rating
A reminder: this is an assessment of how likely a company is to repay its debts, issued by agencies like S&P. A lower rating usually means it costs the company more to borrow money.
Capital expenditure (capex)
A reminder: capex is money spent on long-term assets like data centres and chips. High capex can boost future growth but reduces cash available today.
Free cash flow
A reminder: free cash flow is the cash a company has left after paying for operations and capex. Falling free cash flow despite rising revenue is what worries ratings agencies.
Why it matters for you

Credit rating moves on big tech firms show how seriously investors are weighing AI spending risk, a useful example for finance interviews discussing how to assess a company's financial health beyond its headline revenue growth.

Startups & funding

Aramco pays $2.6 billion for 49% of Saudi AI firm Humain

Saudi oil giant Aramco takes a near-half stake in state-backed AI company Humain, while sovereign fund PIF keeps majority control.

Saudi Aramco has closed a $2.6 billion deal to acquire a 49% stake in Humain, the kingdom's AI champion, according to GamesBeat and Startup Fortune. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund (PIF) retains the remaining 51%, keeping majority control.

Humain was set up as part of Saudi Arabia's push to build its own domestic AI capabilities, covering everything from models to infrastructure, as the kingdom looks to diversify its economy beyond oil.

The deal brings Aramco's cash and industrial assets together with Humain's AI ambitions, echoing a wider pattern of Gulf oil wealth being funnelled directly into AI champions, similar to ADNOC's tie-up with OpenAI and G42.

Sources: GamesBeat · Startup Fortune · Unite.AI

Key concepts in this story
Equity stake valuation gain/loss
A reminder: this is the change in value of a company's ownership stake in another business. If Humain's value rises after the deal, Aramco's $2.6 billion stake becomes worth more on paper.
Full AI stack
A reminder: Humain is reportedly building AI capability across multiple layers, from infrastructure to models, rather than specialising in just one part of the technology.
Why it matters for you

This deal shows how Gulf states are using oil profits to build sovereign AI champions rather than just funding Western labs, a pattern worth knowing if you're tracking where global AI capital and control are heading.

Startups & funding

Deepgram acquires voice AI startup Daily, maker of Pipecat

Speech recognition company Deepgram buys Daily, whose open-source framework Pipecat helps developers build voice AI agents.

Voice AI company Deepgram has acquired Daily, the startup behind the open-source voice AI framework Pipecat, according to AI Insider.

Pipecat is used by developers to build real-time voice applications, such as AI agents that can hold spoken conversations, by connecting speech recognition, language models and text-to-speech tools together.

The acquisition brings Deepgram, known for speech-to-text technology, closer to offering a complete toolkit for building voice-based AI agents, rather than just one piece of the pipeline.

Sources: AI Insider

Key concepts in this story
Open source
A reminder: open-source software has its code made publicly available, so anyone can use, inspect or modify it, as with Pipecat's framework for building voice AI tools.
API (application programming interface)
A reminder: an API is a set way for different software to talk to each other. Pipecat lets developers connect an API for speech recognition, an API for an LLM, and an API for speech generation into one voice assistant.
Why it matters for you

Voice AI is becoming a distinct product category, so understanding frameworks like Pipecat is useful if you want to build or discuss conversational AI agents in interviews, especially in customer service or fintech voice applications.

Code corner

Simulating an AI agent's JSON response for an oil field sensor alert

Based on: OpenAI signs four Abu Dhabi deals covering oil, government and investment

ADNOC is deploying OpenAI's AI agents to help run oil and gas operations. Real systems call an LLM API and get back structured JSON data that other code can act on. Here's a simplified, offline version showing how that works, using a fake response instead of a real API call.

import json

def fake_llm_response(prompt):
    # Pretend this came back from an LLM API after analysing a sensor log
    return json.dumps({
        "summary": "Pump 4 shows early signs of wear.",
        "action": "schedule_maintenance",
        "urgency": "medium"
    })

prompt = "Analyse sensor log: Pump 4 vibration up 12% this week."
raw_response = fake_llm_response(prompt)
data = json.loads(raw_response)

print(f"Summary: {data['summary']}")
print(f"Action recommended: {data['action']}")
print(f"Urgency: {data['urgency']}")

if data["urgency"] == "medium":
    print("Flagging to human engineer for review.")
elif data["urgency"] == "high":
    print("Triggering immediate shutdown protocol.")
else:
    print("No action needed right now.")
Line by line
  1. `fake_llm_response` stands in for a real API call to an LLM, returning a JSON string just like a real AI agent might after analysing sensor data.
  2. `json.dumps(...)` converts a Python dictionary into a JSON-formatted string, mimicking what an API would send over the network.
  3. `json.loads(raw_response)` parses that JSON string back into a Python dictionary so we can use its values in code.
  4. The f-strings print out specific fields from the dictionary, such as `data['action']`, showing how code reads structured AI output rather than plain text.
  5. The if/elif/else block shows how an application decides what to do next based on the AI's urgency rating, which is the core idea behind an AI agent taking action.

Try it: Change the urgency value to "high" in fake_llm_response and run the code again to see the shutdown branch trigger. Then try adding a new field like "confidence": 0.82 to the dictionary and print it out.
