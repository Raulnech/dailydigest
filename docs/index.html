The AI industry

White House set to crown Nvidia, Microsoft and OpenAI as AI 'national champions'

A Wall Street Journal report says the Trump administration plans to formally designate more than two dozen firms as strategically vital to America's AI race.

The Trump administration is reportedly preparing to name Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon, OpenAI, Anthropic and more than twenty other companies as US 'national champions' in artificial intelligence, according to the Wall Street Journal and multiple outlets citing that report. The designation would mark these firms as central to US AI strategy, though exact details of what status brings remain unclear from the reporting so far.

The move fits a wider pattern of governments treating AI capability as a matter of national security rather than just business. Washington has already used export controls — rules restricting which countries and buyers can receive advanced chips — to keep cutting-edge hardware out of rivals' hands. Naming firms as champions would extend that logic, framing their success as America's success in a global contest, much like how countries pursue sovereign AI: building or controlling AI capability within their own borders rather than relying on foreign providers.

What remains uncertain is whether the label brings concrete benefits, such as streamlined permits for data centres or preferential access to export licences, or is mainly symbolic. Critics may ask whether cosying up to a handful of giants risks crowding out smaller competitors. For now, the story is a signal of intent: AI is being treated as critical infrastructure, on par with energy or defence.

Sources: Reuters · Open Magazine · Crypto Briefing

Key concepts in this story
National champion
A 'national champion' is a company a government formally treats as strategically important to the country's economic or security interests, sometimes receiving favourable treatment in return for helping national goals.
Export controls
Export controls are government rules limiting which countries or companies can buy certain technology, such as advanced AI chips, usually to stop rivals gaining military or economic advantage.
Sovereign AI
Sovereign AI means a country building or controlling its own AI systems and infrastructure rather than depending entirely on foreign companies for the technology.
Why it matters for you

If this designation becomes real policy, it could mean faster approvals, funding, or chip access for workers at these firms — handy to mention if you're interviewing at any of the named companies. It also signals that understanding AI policy and export rules is becoming as important as understanding the technology itself.

The AI industry

Nvidia's supply guarantees balloon to $530bn in a single quarter

Nvidia's commitments to guarantee future chip supply jumped by roughly a quarter of a trillion dollars in just three months, according to Tom's Hardware.

Nvidia has reportedly built up $530 billion in financial guarantees tied to securing future supply of its AI chips, with that figure climbing by almost $250 billion in a single quarter, according to Tom's Hardware. The scale of the jump suggests Nvidia is locking in capacity from chip manufacturers well ahead of demand, aiming to avoid the supply shortages that have dogged the AI chip market in recent years.

These guarantees function similarly to a contract backlog: promises of future business that give manufacturers confidence to expand factories, even though the cash hasn't changed hands yet. For a company at the centre of the AI boom, securing supply is arguably as important as selling the chips themselves, since any shortfall could hand business to rivals.

Sources: Tom's Hardware

Key concepts in this story
Supply commitment / guarantee
A supply guarantee is a promise to buy or pay for future production, giving manufacturers the confidence to build capacity now, rather like pre-ordering a year's worth of flour so your baker invests in a bigger oven.
Contract backlog
A contract backlog is the total value of orders a company has agreed to but not yet delivered or paid out — a sign of future revenue, but not guaranteed cash yet.
Why it matters for you

Supply chain commitments of this size show how dependent the entire AI industry is on chip availability, a useful fact for anyone analysing AI company balance sheets or discussing capital expenditure in an interview.

The AI industry

Daikin unveils 60MW cooling blueprint for Nvidia AI data centres

The Japanese air-conditioning giant has released a liquid-cooling reference design aimed squarely at Nvidia-powered AI data centres.

Daikin has introduced a reference design for liquid cooling systems capable of handling 60 megawatts of capacity, specifically built for Nvidia-based AI data centres, according to Digitimes. Liquid cooling circulates fluid directly around hot components, removing heat far more efficiently than traditional air conditioning.

As AI chips get more powerful, they generate far more heat in smaller spaces, making cooling a bottleneck for building bigger data centres. A standardised 'reference design' lets data centre operators copy a proven blueprint rather than engineering cooling systems from scratch, potentially speeding up construction of the next wave of AI infrastructure.

Sources: digitimes

Key concepts in this story
Liquid cooling
Liquid cooling uses fluid circulating through or near computer chips to carry heat away faster than air can, similar to how a car radiator uses coolant rather than just airflow.
Megawatt (MW)
A megawatt is a unit of power equal to one million watts; a 60MW data centre design could roughly power tens of thousands of homes, giving a sense of the energy AI infrastructure now demands.
Why it matters for you

Cooling and power are becoming as critical to AI infrastructure careers as chip design itself — engineers and project managers in this space are increasingly in demand alongside software roles.

AI & finance

AI data-centre debt now 31% of investment-grade bond issuance, Fed warns

The Federal Reserve's latest Financial Stability Report flags how much corporate borrowing is now tied to building AI data centres.

The US Federal Reserve's Financial Stability Report found that debt linked to AI data centres now makes up 31% of all investment-grade bond issuance, according to Tech Times. Investment-grade bonds are debt considered relatively safe to lend against, typically issued by large, financially stable companies — so a near-third share going towards AI infrastructure shows how central the sector has become to corporate borrowing.

Central banks like the Fed monitor this kind of concentration because if AI spending slows or expected returns don't materialise, that debt could become harder to repay, potentially rippling through bond markets that pension funds and insurers rely on. This echoes wider worries about an AI bubble, where asset prices and borrowing run ahead of actual, provable returns.

The report doesn't say the debt is unsustainable, but flagging the scale is itself notable: it puts AI infrastructure spending squarely in the category of systemic financial risks worth tracking, alongside things like excessive leverage or shaky underwriting standards. Expect more scrutiny of who is lending to data centre builders, and on what terms, in the months ahead.

Sources: Tech Times

Key concepts in this story
Investment-grade bond
An investment-grade bond is debt issued by borrowers judged relatively low-risk by credit rating agencies, as opposed to riskier 'high-yield' or 'junk' bonds.
AI bubble
An AI bubble refers to the fear that AI-related valuations and spending have risen faster than the actual profits being generated, similar to the dot-com bubble of the early 2000s.
Central bank
A central bank, like the US Federal Reserve, oversees a country's money supply and monitors risks to the financial system, stepping in when problems could spread across banks and markets.
Why it matters for you

Spotting debt concentration like this is exactly the kind of insight a junior analyst is expected to flag in a credit or risk team — knowing how to read a central bank stability report is a genuinely useful interview talking point.

SpaceX prices shares at $135 ahead of record-breaking IPO

SpaceX is set to price what could become the largest initial public offering in history, according to its regulatory filing.

SpaceX is reportedly pricing its shares at $135 ahead of an initial public offering, or IPO — the first time a private company sells shares to the public on a stock exchange — in what TradingKey describes as potentially the largest IPO ever recorded. The pricing comes from SpaceX's S-1, the detailed financial filing companies must submit to regulators before going public.

Pricing an IPO involves balancing investor demand against how much capital the company wants to raise, while setting a value that allows the stock to perform well once trading begins. A hugely oversubscribed IPO, where demand for shares exceeds the supply on offer, often pushes prices up and signals strong investor confidence, though it can also indicate hype running ahead of fundamentals.

If this IPO proceeds as described, it would represent a landmark moment for both the space industry and public markets, giving ordinary investors a chance to own a slice of a company previously reserved for private investors and funds. Analysts will be watching closely to see how the stock trades once shares actually hit the market.

Sources: TradingKey

Key concepts in this story
IPO (initial public offering)
An IPO is when a private company sells shares to the public for the first time, listing on a stock exchange so anyone can buy and sell its stock.
S-1 filing
An S-1 is a detailed document US companies must file with regulators before an IPO, disclosing financials, risks, and business details so investors can make informed decisions.
Oversubscribed
An IPO is 'oversubscribed' when investors want to buy more shares than are actually being offered, often pushing the final price higher than initially expected.
Why it matters for you

Understanding how IPO pricing and filings work is core knowledge for anyone wanting to work in equity research, investment banking, or just make sense of why a stock pops or flops on its first trading day.

AI & finance

Anthropic and OpenAI's rival numbers lay bare the cost of the AI race

A comparison of revenue, valuation and losses shows just how expensive the fight for AI dominance between OpenAI and Anthropic has become.

A report from The Financial Express compares OpenAI and Anthropic across revenue, valuation and losses, highlighting how both leading AI labs are spending enormous sums to compete for dominance in the sector. Both companies have achieved high valuations — the price investors assign to a private company based on expected future growth — despite reportedly running significant losses.

This pattern is common among frontier AI labs: companies racing to build the most capable models often prioritise growth and market share over near-term profit, betting that today's spending will secure tomorrow's dominant position. Investors tolerate the losses because they're betting on enormous future upside, similar to how early e-commerce firms burned cash for years before turning profitable.

The rivalry matters beyond headlines because it shapes how much computing power, talent and capital gets funnelled into AI development, and who ultimately controls the most widely used AI products. Whoever wins market share now may have significant pricing power and influence over how AI tools get built and used for years to come.

Sources: The Financial Express

Key concepts in this story
Valuation
Valuation is the estimated worth of a company, often based on what investors are willing to pay for a stake rather than current profits — a company can be valued at billions even while losing money, if investors believe future growth will be huge.
Frontier AI lab
A frontier AI lab is an organisation, like OpenAI or Anthropic, working at the cutting edge of what AI models can do, often spending heavily on research and computing power to stay ahead.
Revenue growth rate
Revenue growth rate measures how quickly a company's sales are increasing over time, often used by investors to judge whether high losses are justified by rapid expansion.
Why it matters for you

Comparing financials between rival AI labs is a useful skill for finance and strategy roles, and a great way to show in an interview that you understand the difference between hype, valuation and actual business performance.

Startups & funding

Plural raises €1bn fund to back Europe's defence, energy and AI 'moonshots'

The operator-led venture capital firm behind early bets on Helsing has closed its largest fund yet, aiming to fuel ambitious European startups.

London-based venture capital firm Plural has closed a €1 billion (roughly $1.1 billion) fund to invest in early-stage European startups, according to TechCrunch and Crunchbase News. Two-thirds of the money will go into Plural's third early-stage fund, while the remaining third forms a new 'opportunities' fund for follow-on investments in existing portfolio companies as they grow.

Plural's portfolio spans energy, defence, robotics, health and AI infrastructure, including ambitious projects like fusion energy startup Proxima Fusion and defence company Helsing. Partner Khaled Helioui described these as 'moonshots' — bets on capital-intensive, high-risk ventures that could take years to pay off but could have outsized impact if they succeed. Unlike typical early-stage VC bets, some of these projects reportedly need hundreds of millions of dollars just to get off the ground.

The fund reflects a broader shift in European venture capital: firms are growing larger to keep pace with founders, especially repeat entrepreneurs, who are increasingly tackling capital-intensive sectors tied to European sovereignty and resilience, rather than just building another software app. Plural also deliberately raised more capital from European investors, a sign of growing confidence in homegrown tech ambitions, particularly in countries closer to Russia's border, where urgency around defence and energy independence runs higher.

Sources: TechCrunch · Crunchbase News · finway.com.ua

Key concepts in this story
Early-stage vs growth fund
Early-stage funds invest in young, unproven startups, usually taking higher risk for a bigger potential reward, while growth or follow-on funds invest more cash into companies that have already shown promise.
Follow-on investment
A follow-on investment is additional money a VC firm puts into a startup it has already backed, often in a later funding round, to maintain its ownership stake as the company grows.
Limited partner (LP)
A limited partner is an investor — often a pension fund, university endowment or wealthy individual — who provides capital to a venture capital fund but doesn't make day-to-day investment decisions.
Why it matters for you

Big European funds like this are creating more job opportunities in venture capital and startup operations outside Silicon Valley, worth knowing if you're considering a career path beyond the usual US tech hubs.

Startups & funding

OpenAI buys drug-discovery startup Orchestra Bio to build life-sciences AI

OpenAI has acquired Orchestra Bio, folding its team into a new push to build AI tools for drug development under the Rosalind effort.

OpenAI has acquired Orchestra Bio, a drug-development startup that had previously raised around $20 million, according to NewsBytes and The Economic Times. The Orchestra Bio team will now work on OpenAI's life-sciences AI efforts, reportedly tied to a project called Rosalind.

The move fits a pattern of major AI labs acquiring smaller specialised startups, sometimes described as acqui-hires, to quickly gain expertise in a particular field rather than building that knowledge from scratch. Drug discovery is a prime example of vertical AI: applying general AI techniques to a narrow, high-value domain where deep scientific knowledge matters as much as the underlying model.

By moving into life sciences, OpenAI is signalling ambitions beyond chatbots and coding assistants, aiming to apply its models to scientific problems like identifying promising drug candidates faster and cheaper than traditional lab methods allow.

Sources: NewsBytes · The Economic Times

Key concepts in this story
Acquisition
An acquisition is when one company buys another, taking over its assets, technology and usually its staff — here OpenAI bought Orchestra Bio outright rather than just hiring individuals.
Vertical AI
Vertical AI means applying AI to a specific industry or problem, like drug discovery or legal contracts, rather than building general-purpose tools meant for many different uses.
Why it matters for you

This shows a growing demand for people who combine domain expertise, like biology or chemistry, with AI skills — a strong pitch for science graduates moving into tech, and a reminder that AI jobs aren't limited to pure software roles.

Code corner

Pricing a hypothetical IPO

Based on: SpaceX prices shares at $135 ahead of record-breaking IPO

Today's SpaceX story is about IPO pricing — let's build a tiny Python example that calculates a company's market capitalisation from its share price and shares outstanding, plus how oversubscribed an offering was. We'll use a fictional example company rather than real SpaceX figures, since those weren't given in the source.

def market_cap(price_per_share, shares_outstanding):
    return price_per_share * shares_outstanding

def oversubscription_ratio(shares_demanded, shares_offered):
    return shares_demanded / shares_offered

# Example: a fictional company 'ExampleCorp'
price_per_share = 50          # dollars
shares_outstanding = 20_000_000
shares_offered = 2_000_000
shares_demanded = 7_000_000

cap = market_cap(price_per_share, shares_outstanding)
ratio = oversubscription_ratio(shares_demanded, shares_offered)

print(f"Market cap: ${cap:,.0f}")
print(f"Oversubscription ratio: {ratio:.1f}x")

if ratio > 1:
    print("Demand exceeded supply — the IPO was oversubscribed.")
else:
    print("Demand did not exceed supply.")
Line by line
  1. `def market_cap(...)` defines a function that multiplies share price by total shares outstanding to estimate a company's total value.
  2. `def oversubscription_ratio(...)` divides the number of shares investors wanted by the number actually offered, showing how much demand outstripped supply.
  3. The example variables set a fictional price, share counts and demand figures — swap these for real numbers from an actual IPO filing if you have them.
  4. The f-strings (`f"..."`) format numbers nicely, using `,.0f` to add thousand separators and no decimals for the market cap.
  5. The `if ratio > 1` check prints a plain-English message explaining whether the offering was oversubscribed.

Try it: Change shares_demanded to 1,000,000 (less than shares_offered) and re-run the code — notice how the message changes to say demand did not exceed supply, and think about what that would mean for the share price on debut.
