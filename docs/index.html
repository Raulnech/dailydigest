The AI industry

OpenAI says ChatGPT search volume now a fifth of Google's

OpenAI's consumer chief claims ChatGPT handles 20% as many search-style queries as Google, as the chatbot leans harder into ads ahead of a possible IPO.

OpenAI revealed at a press event this week that the number of search-style queries typed into ChatGPT now equals roughly 20% of the volume handled by Google. Adam Fry, who leads consumer product for ChatGPT, defined a 'search' as any query that triggers a live web lookup, rather than a simple definition or how-to answer. He did not say where OpenAI got its Google figure from.

Context matters here: Microsoft's Bing took over a decade to reach just 3% of the search market, so a reported 20% share for ChatGPT, even measured on OpenAI's own terms, marks a striking shift. Google has responded by pulling back on sending traffic to outside websites and building more AI features of its own, including AI Overviews and personalised results with charts. OpenAI, meanwhile, is pitching what it calls 'Intelligent UI' — interactive, visual answers rather than plain text.

The bigger story is money. Much like Google, OpenAI is eyeing advertising as a route to profitability, especially with an IPO reportedly being considered for early next year. The company has already begun showing images of sponsored products inside ChatGPT rather than just text links, and Fry said user pushback has been minimal so far — a signal that more ad formats could be on the way.

Sources: The Verge

Key concepts in this story
Market share
The slice of total activity in a market that one company captures — if ChatGPT handles 20 units of search for every 100 Google handles, it has roughly a 17% share of that combined market.
Generative search
Reminder: search results generated as a written AI answer rather than a list of links, often pulling in several sources at once.
Ad-supported business model
A product is free or cheap to use because the company earns money by showing adverts instead, the way Google Search and now ChatGPT are starting to do.
Why it matters for you

Search is one of the few proven ways to make huge sums from consumer AI, so expect more companies hiring for ad-tech and product roles inside AI labs. In interviews, be ready to explain why 'engagement' numbers alone don't pay the bills — advertising or subscriptions do.

The AI industry

Meta seeks up to $25bn in bond sale to fund AI build-out

Days after a ratings agency turned negative on its outlook, Meta is tapping debt markets for up to $25bn to keep funding data centres and AI.

Meta is raising between $20bn and $25bn through a bond sale, split roughly two-thirds in US dollars and one-third in euros, according to people familiar with the deal. The move follows S&P Global Ratings cutting its outlook on Meta from 'AA- stable' to 'AA- negative', flagging uncertainty over the company's huge capital spending and negative cash flow.

Meta's latest earnings showed just how much it is spending: full-year capital expenditure, the money spent on buildings, chips and equipment, is now guided at $135bn to $145bn, up $5bn from before. The company posted negative free cash flow of $2.12bn last quarter, meaning it spent more cash than it brought in, joining Alphabet and Amazon in the same position. Meta already raised $25bn in bonds back in April and has said it wants $95bn more for AI — $35bn through selling new shares and $60bn through a gradual share-sale programme.

Investors reacted calmly: Meta's stock rose 2.7% even as the new debt sale was announced, clawing back some ground after its worst month in four years. The episode shows how even the most profitable tech giants are leaning on borrowed money, not just their own profits, to pay for the AI race — a pattern that raises the stakes if AI spending doesn't pay off as quickly as hoped.

Sources: Tech

Key concepts in this story
Bond
A loan investors make to a company in exchange for regular interest payments and their money back later; buying a Meta bond means lending Meta cash for a promised return.
Credit rating outlook
A forward-looking signal from a ratings agency about where a company's credit rating might head next — 'negative outlook' means a downgrade is more likely soon, even if the rating itself hasn't changed yet.
At-the-market offering
Reminder: a way of selling new shares gradually into the stock market over time, rather than all at once, to avoid shocking the share price.
Why it matters for you

Understanding why a profitable company still borrows billions is a classic finance interview question — it shows you grasp the gap between accounting profit and actual cash in the bank. It also links directly to the Nikkei story below on how much hidden debt the whole industry is taking on.

The AI industry

AMD shares jump 8% as data centre revenue nearly doubles

AMD beat Wall Street forecasts as AI chip demand drove data centre revenue up sharply, even as its PC and gaming business shrank.

AMD reported third-quarter revenue of $13.38bn, up 45% year-on-year and ahead of analyst expectations, with adjusted earnings per share of $2.09 versus the $1.95 forecast. Data centre sales nearly doubled from a year earlier and now make up 64% of AMD's total revenue, up from 47% a year ago. The stock jumped 8% in after-hours trading.

Chief executive Lisa Su said she expects server revenue to double again in 2027, crediting rising demand for AI chips and the company's push into 'agentic AI' — software that can carry out multi-step tasks on its own. AMD's server processors are important here because they can run continuously for hours to support these AI agents. The company's rack-scale AI systems, called Helios, are now being used by Meta, Microsoft, Anthropic, OpenAI and Oracle, directly challenging Nvidia's equivalent hardware.

Not every part of the business is thriving: combined PC and gaming revenue fell 8%, with gaming sales down 40%, as the broader PC market struggles with pricier components. AMD's results contrast with rival Intel, whose stock plunged 29% last week despite similar data centre growth, on fears it can't keep up with demand. AMD forecasts about $17bn in revenue for the current quarter, ahead of estimates, and in September it agreed to buy AI start-up World Labs for $8.2bn to build so-called world models for robotics.

Sources: Tech

Key concepts in this story
Earnings per share (EPS)
Reminder: a company's profit divided by its number of shares outstanding, used to judge whether results beat or missed expectations.
GPU vs CPU
A GPU (graphics processing unit) is built to do many simple calculations at once, which suits training AI models, while a CPU (central processing unit) handles varied, sequential tasks — useful for running AI agents that make decisions step by step.
World model
Reminder: an AI system that simulates how a 3D environment behaves, letting robots or other physical systems practise and plan before acting in the real world.
Why it matters for you

Chip results are a leading indicator of how much money is actually flowing into AI infrastructure, which matters for anyone pricing AI stocks or pitching an AI-adjacent business. Knowing the difference between GPU and CPU demand is a useful, concrete detail to drop into a technical interview.

AI & finance

Anthropic files for Nasdaq IPO under ticker ANT

Anthropic has filed to list on the Nasdaq, setting up what could be the largest pure-play AI stock market debut yet.

Anthropic, the maker of the Claude chatbot, has filed paperwork to go public on the Nasdaq exchange under the ticker symbol ANT, according to multiple reports. The filing would make Anthropic one of the first major AI labs to list its shares directly on public markets, rather than raising money only from private investors.

Commentators have noted the listing could cast a long shadow over privately held AI companies, because a public share price gives investors a real-time benchmark to judge whether sky-high private valuations — including Anthropic's own — are justified. AMD has already committed to invest up to $5bn in Anthropic, underlining how closely intertwined chipmakers and AI labs have become.

Further detail on pricing, timing and the amount Anthropic hopes to raise has not yet been confirmed, but the filing is widely seen as a milestone moment for the AI industry's relationship with public markets.

Sources: Crypto Briefing · Yahoo Finance · Morningstar

Key concepts in this story
IPO (initial public offering)
Reminder: the first time a company sells shares to the general public on a stock exchange, turning a private company into a publicly traded one.
Pure-play company
A business focused on a single industry or product line rather than many — Anthropic is called 'pure-play AI' because, unlike Google or Amazon, almost all its revenue comes from AI products.
Valuation
Reminder: an estimate of what a company is worth, based on its revenue, growth and investor demand; an IPO price effectively sets a new, public valuation.
Why it matters for you

A high-profile AI IPO will be closely watched by anyone working in finance or AI, since its share price becomes a reference point for valuing every other AI start-up. It's a good topic to bring up in interviews to show you follow how private AI hype translates — or doesn't — into public market value.

AI & finance

Tech giants' off-balance-sheet AI debt reportedly jumps 90% to $3.2tn

Hidden borrowing tied to AI infrastructure projects has nearly doubled in six months, according to Nikkei Asia.

US tech giants' off-balance-sheet debt linked to AI has reportedly swollen 90% over six months to reach $3.2tn, according to Nikkei Asia. This type of debt is often arranged through separate financing vehicles for data centres and chips, so it doesn't show up directly on a company's main balance sheet even though the company may still be on the hook for it.

Few further details were given, but the figure adds to growing concern about how much borrowed money, rather than company profits, is fuelling the AI build-out — a theme that echoes Meta's bond sale and Apollo's AI lending push reported elsewhere today.

Sources: Nikkei Asia

Key concepts in this story
Off-balance-sheet debt
Borrowing structured through a separate entity or project so it doesn't appear as a direct liability on the parent company's own balance sheet, even though the company may still bear the financial risk.
Contagion risk
Reminder: the danger that trouble at one company or lender spreads to others connected to it, the way a default on one AI data-centre loan could ripple through its lenders and investors.
Why it matters for you

If you're analysing AI companies' financial health, off-balance-sheet debt is exactly the kind of figure that doesn't show up in a quick glance at a balance sheet — knowing to look for it is a sharp signal in a finance interview.

AI & finance

Apollo's AI lending book reportedly rivals big banks

Private equity giant Apollo is reportedly building a record book of loans tied to AI infrastructure, challenging traditional bank lenders.

Asset manager Apollo has reportedly built a record volume of lending tied to AI infrastructure projects, putting it in direct competition with major banks for this business, according to PYMNTS.com. Few specifics were given in the available report, but it fits a broader pattern of non-bank lenders stepping in to finance the enormous cost of AI data centres and chips.

This matters because private lenders often move faster and take on more risk than regulated banks, but with less public disclosure — making it harder for outsiders to judge how much risk is building up in the system.

Sources: PYMNTS.com

Key concepts in this story
Private credit
Lending done by investment firms rather than regulated banks, often to companies or projects that want faster or more flexible financing than a bank would offer.
Non-bank lender
Any institution, like a private equity firm or hedge fund, that lends money outside the traditional, regulated banking system — Apollo is an example.
Why it matters for you

The rise of private lenders in AI financing is reshaping who holds the risk if the AI spending boom slows down, which is a sharp topic to raise when discussing systemic risk in a finance interview.

Startups & funding

Fal doubles valuation to $16bn in $1.3bn funding round

The generative media inference start-up fal has raised $1.3bn, doubling its valuation as demand for running AI models in production surges.

Fal, a start-up that specialises in running generative AI models for images, video and audio at scale, has raised $1.3bn in new funding at a $16bn valuation — roughly double its previous mark, according to multiple reports. The company focuses specifically on inference, the step where a trained AI model is actually used to generate an output, rather than on building or training the models themselves.

The scale of the round reflects how much investor money is chasing the 'plumbing' layer of AI — the infrastructure that makes running models fast and cheap — rather than just the headline-grabbing model makers. Further details on investors or the company's revenue were not disclosed in the available reports.

Sources: SiliconANGLE · Bloomberg.com · Startup Fortune

Key concepts in this story
Inference
Reminder: the stage where an already-trained AI model is actually used to answer a question or generate an image, as opposed to training, when the model first learns from data.
Up round
Reminder: a funding round where a start-up is valued higher than in its previous round — fal's valuation doubling to $16bn is a clear example.
Why it matters for you

Fal's rise shows that huge money is flowing not just to flashy chatbots but to unglamorous infrastructure that makes AI products actually work at scale — a useful angle if you're job-hunting in AI infrastructure or MLOps rather than model research.

Startups & funding

CrowdStrike to spend nearly $500m on two AI security start-ups

Cybersecurity firm CrowdStrike is buying two start-ups for roughly $495m as the market for securing AI agents takes off.

CrowdStrike has agreed to acquire two AI security start-ups for a combined price of close to $500m, according to multiple reports. The deals come as companies increasingly deploy AI agents — software that can take actions on its own, like booking, coding or making purchases — and need new tools to stop them being hijacked or misused.

Details on the specific start-ups and what their products do were limited in the available reports, but the move fits a pattern: as agentic AI spreads into businesses, demand is growing fast for specialist firms that can monitor, test and lock down these systems before something goes wrong.

Sources: TechCrunch · BankInfoSecurity · SC Media

Key concepts in this story
Cybersecurity
The practice of protecting computer systems, networks and data from unauthorised access or attack — increasingly extended to cover AI systems and the agents they run.
Acquisition
Reminder: when one company buys another outright, typically to gain its technology, customers or talent rather than building the same thing from scratch.
Why it matters for you

Securing AI agents is becoming its own job category, blending traditional cybersecurity skills with an understanding of how AI models and agents actually work — a strong niche to mention if you're coming from a security background into AI.

Code corner

Simulating a 'search classifier' API response

Based on: OpenAI says ChatGPT search volume now a fifth of Google's

OpenAI said it counts a ChatGPT interaction as a 'search' only if it triggers a live web lookup. Let's mimic how an app might call an AI API to classify queries and then work out what share of total queries counted as searches — using a fake response instead of a real API key.

import json

def fake_llm_classify(query):
    # Pretend this came back from an LLM API call
    search_keywords = ["latest", "news", "price", "who is", "weather"]
    is_search = any(word in query.lower() for word in search_keywords)
    return json.dumps({"query": query, "is_search": is_search})

queries = [
    "What's the latest Nvidia stock price?",
    "Define capital expenditure",
    "Who is the CEO of Anthropic?",
    "Write a haiku about chips",
    "weather in London today",
]

results = [json.loads(fake_llm_classify(q)) for q in queries]
search_count = sum(1 for r in results if r["is_search"])
total = len(results)
ratio = search_count / total

for r in results:
    print(r)

print(f"Search share: {ratio:.0%} of {total} queries")
Line by line
  1. `import json` lets us turn Python dictionaries into text (and back), the same format real APIs use to send responses.
  2. `fake_llm_classify` stands in for a real API call: instead of sending the query to OpenAI's servers, it checks for keywords and returns a JSON string, just like a real API response would look.
  3. `json.dumps` converts our Python dictionary into a JSON string; `json.loads` later converts it back into a dictionary so we can use the values.
  4. The list comprehension `[json.loads(...) for q in queries]` runs the fake classifier on every query and collects the parsed results.
  5. `search_count` adds up how many results were flagged as searches, and dividing by `total` gives the search share, printed as a percentage with `:.0%`.

Try it: Add three of your own queries to the `queries` list, including at least one that should NOT count as a search, and check whether the printed `ratio` matches what you expect.
