The AI industry

Nvidia halts all AI chip shipments to China, even the ones Washington approved

Interim chief Colette Kress confirms Nvidia has stopped sending licensed H200 processors to Chinese buyers, reversing a fight the company itself won last December.

Nvidia has stopped shipping its advanced H200 artificial intelligence chips to China, despite having secured explicit permission from the White House to do so less than a year ago. The disclosure came from interim CEO Colette Kress during Tuesday's earnings call, who said guidance for coming quarters assumes zero revenue from licensed Hopper-generation data centre chips sold into China.

The H200 is part of Nvidia's Hopper chip architecture, essentially a generation or 'family' of processor designs built for training and running AI models. Washington restricts exports of the most powerful chips to China through a licensing system designed to slow Chinese military and AI progress; Nvidia had spent much of the past two years lobbying for permission to keep selling a cut-down, export-compliant version. Getting that approval last December was seen as a win for then-CEO Jensen Huang. Kress gave no reason for the about-turn, and didn't say whether Nvidia would pursue other China business.

The shift follows Huang's abrupt departure last week amid reports of personal scandal, and separate difficulties getting chips into China at all: Reuters has reported that roughly 600,000 H200 units are stuck in Hong Kong because Beijing is withholding import approval, part of a broader push by China to wean itself off US chip technology. The result is that Nvidia, the world's most valuable chipmaker, is now locked out of a market it fought hard to keep.

Sources: The Verge · Reuters · Bloomberg.com

Key concepts in this story
Export licence
A government permit allowing a company to sell a restricted product abroad. Nvidia needed one to ship advanced chips to China legally, even after getting approval it can later choose not to use it.
Chip architecture generation
Chipmakers design processors in 'families' that share an underlying blueprint, like Hopper or its successor Blackwell. The H200 belongs to the Hopper family, which is older and easier for regulators to approve for export than newer designs.
Export controls (reminder)
Rules restricting which countries or companies can buy sensitive technology, used here by the US to slow China's access to cutting-edge AI chips.
Why it matters for you

If you work in chip supply chains, trade compliance or equity research, this shows how fast export policy and corporate strategy can diverge from each other, and why geopolitics now sits inside every semiconductor earnings call.

The AI industry

Amazon set for nearly 15% of Anthropic after IPO, with room to nearly double it

Filings show Amazon and Google will together hold close to 30% of the AI firm once it lists, largely through old investments converting into new shares.

Anthropic's IPO filing reveals that Amazon will own just under 15% of the company at listing, while Google will hold close to 14%, making the two cloud giants collectively the biggest shareholders outside Anthropic's founders. Both stakes come in the form of non-voting shares, meaning the companies get the financial upside without a vote on Anthropic's board.

The shares originate from promissory notes, essentially IOUs that convert into equity later, which Amazon and Google received when they invested in Anthropic back in 2023 and 2024, long before its coding tool Claude Code made it a serious rival to OpenAI. Amazon's notes convert at roughly $2.09 to $5.05 per share depending on when they were issued; converting all of them today would push its stake to nearly 24%. It has chosen to convert only part of its holding for now. A consortium including AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC Partners has also agreed to buy $26 billion of this non-voting stock through a private placement, a direct sale of shares outside the public offering.

None of this counts the commercial side: Anthropic has committed to spend more than $100 billion on Amazon's cloud division over the next decade. For Amazon, that means profiting both as an investor and as Anthropic's landlord, a dual position that will be closely watched by investors weighing how much of Big Tech's AI enthusiasm is really about securing captive customers.

Sources: TechCrunch

Key concepts in this story
Non-voting shares
Shares that pay out financially like ordinary stock but give the holder no say in company decisions, letting investors profit without gaining control.
Promissory note / convertible note
An early-stage IOU that can later be exchanged for company shares at a pre-agreed price, letting investors lock in cheap equity before a company is publicly valued.
Private placement
Selling shares directly to a small group of chosen investors rather than to the public market, often used alongside an IPO to secure strategic backers.
Why it matters for you

Understanding how cloud providers quietly become major shareholders in the AI labs they host explains a recurring pattern in this market, and it's a sharp interview talking point about conflicts between being an investor and a supplier.

The AI industry

Microsoft launches seven in-house AI models to undercut OpenAI and Anthropic

New coding and cybersecurity models are pitched as matching rivals' performance at a fraction of the cost.

Microsoft used its Ignite conference to unveil seven AI models built by its own research teams, aimed squarely at reducing its reliance on partners OpenAI and Anthropic. Among them is a coding model Microsoft says performs similarly to Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 while costing only around 5% as much to run.

The move signals Microsoft's push for 'price-performance': getting comparable quality from a model at a much lower running cost, known as inference cost. Rather than competing purely on raw capability, Microsoft appears to be targeting specialised tasks like coding and cybersecurity, where a smaller, more efficient model can match a larger general-purpose rival for a fraction of the compute bill.

The strategy reflects a wider industry trend of labs building narrower, cheaper models for specific jobs rather than only chasing bigger, more expensive general-purpose systems. For Microsoft, which has invested billions in OpenAI, in-house models reduce dependence on external suppliers and give it more pricing control as AI spending comes under growing investor scrutiny.

Sources: Reuters · BigGo Finance · Axios

Key concepts in this story
Price-performance
A measure of how much capability you get per pound or dollar spent, not just raw quality. A model that's 95% as good but costs 20 times less can have far better price-performance.
Narrow (specialised) AI model (reminder)
A model built and tuned for a specific task, like writing code, rather than trying to handle everything a general-purpose model does.
Inference cost
The computing expense of actually running a trained model to answer a query, as opposed to the one-off cost of training it. Cheaper inference means a company can serve more users profitably.
Why it matters for you

This is the clearest sign yet that the AI price war is shifting from training breakthroughs to efficient deployment, a trend worth mentioning if you're asked in an interview how AI companies plan to become profitable.

AI & finance

SEC opens civil probe into Nvidia disclosures as leadership crisis deepens

Regulators are reportedly examining what Nvidia told investors, days after Jensen Huang's abrupt exit.

The US Securities and Exchange Commission has reportedly opened a civil investigation into Nvidia's public disclosures, according to the Wall Street Journal, adding regulatory pressure to a company already dealing with a leadership vacuum following Jensen Huang's resignation.

A civil probe is distinct from a criminal investigation: it focuses on whether a company broke securities rules, such as failing to tell investors about material risks, rather than alleging crimes carrying prison sentences. Penalties can still include heavy fines and demands for corrective disclosures. The exact focus of the SEC's inquiry hasn't been detailed in reporting so far.

The timing is awkward for Nvidia, which is also navigating fallout from its halted China chip sales and questions about its vetting of Chinese buyers. With no permanent CEO in place, investors will be watching closely for how the board handles both the regulatory scrutiny and the broader uncertainty over Nvidia's China strategy.

Sources: BigGo Finance · BigGo Finance · Business Standard

Key concepts in this story
Civil probe
A regulatory investigation into whether rules were broken, typically resulting in fines or forced changes rather than criminal charges, unlike a criminal investigation which can lead to prosecution.
Disclosure obligations
Legal requirements for public companies to tell investors about information that could affect the share price, such as major risks or management changes.
Insider trading (reminder)
Buying or selling shares based on non-public information, which is illegal; SEC probes into disclosures sometimes uncover related insider trading concerns.
Why it matters for you

Knowing the difference between civil and criminal regulatory action is a basic but often-tested piece of financial literacy, useful if you end up in compliance, legal or investor relations roles.

AI & finance

ECB compares AI boom to dotcom bubble as tech firms pile up debt

Europe's central bank flags borrowing by major tech companies as a growing financial stability risk.

The European Central Bank has reportedly drawn parallels between today's AI investment boom and the dotcom bubble of the late 1990s, warning that large technology companies are increasingly funding AI infrastructure with debt rather than cash.

The dotcom bubble saw internet company valuations soar on hype before crashing sharply in 2000, wiping out huge amounts of investor wealth. The ECB's concern is that AI firms' heavy capital spending on data centres and chips, increasingly financed through bond issuance, could leave the wider financial system exposed if AI revenues fail to justify the spending.

This adds to a chorus of warnings this year about an AI bubble, but comes with an added twist: unlike the dotcom era, much of today's spending is debt-financed, meaning a slowdown could hit bond markets and banks, not just shareholders. Central banks like the ECB monitor this kind of systemic risk because trouble at large borrowers can ripple into the broader financial system.

Sources: Euronews.com · Bankingnews

Key concepts in this story
Dotcom bubble
A period around 1999–2000 when internet company share prices soared far beyond their actual earnings, before crashing dramatically when investors realised many had no viable business model.
AI bubble (reminder)
The concern that AI company valuations and spending have outrun realistic near-term profits, risking a sharp correction if expectations aren't met.
Systemic risk
The danger that trouble at one large company or sector spreads through the wider financial system, for example if heavily-indebted tech firms struggling to repay bonds causes losses at the banks holding that debt.
Why it matters for you

Central bank warnings like this shape regulation and lending conditions across the economy, so it's worth being able to explain in an interview why debt-funded AI spending is seen as riskier than cash-funded spending.

AI & finance

Anthropic set to price IPO above $70–78 range as demand surges

Order books are closing with strong demand for the Claude maker's Nasdaq debut under ticker 'ANT', even as broader AI stocks wobble.

Anthropic looks set to price its initial public offering above its initial $70 to $78 per share range, according to multiple sources briefed on the deal, with order books closing on Tuesday ahead of a Nasdaq debut under the ticker ANT on 19 November. One major backer, investor Brad Gerstner of Altimeter, has reportedly said he aims to put around $5 billion into the offering, five times more than he has committed to any previous IPO.

The listing comes roughly five months after SpaceX's record-breaking $85.7 billion IPO, but into a public market now far warier of AI valuations. Anthropic's prospectus shows revenue of $33.24 billion for the nine months to September, up roughly 1,200% year-on-year, driven largely by its Claude Code coding tool. It also reported a $104 billion net loss over the same period, though the company says most of that reflects accounting changes in the fair value of convertible notes and derivative liabilities rather than operating losses.

Founded in 2021 by researchers who left OpenAI, Anthropic has built its reputation on AI safety, and its prospectus includes more than 90 pages of risk factors. CEO Dario Amodei has framed the listing as a step toward solving long-standing scientific problems, while acknowledging the need to manage AI's risks carefully. The IPO is widely seen as a key test of investor appetite in an otherwise jittery AI market.

Sources: Tech · Tech · AP News

Key concepts in this story
Order book (IPO)
The running tally of investor demand for shares before a listing; a large, oversubscribed order book lets a company price its IPO higher than originally planned.
Fair value accounting of convertible notes
Companies must periodically re-estimate what their convertible notes are currently worth; a rising company valuation can paradoxically create large accounting losses on these instruments even though no cash has actually been lost.
Revenue growth rate (reminder)
How fast a company's sales are increasing, usually shown as a percentage versus the same period last year; Anthropic's 1,200% figure shows extremely rapid expansion.
Why it matters for you

The Anthropic listing is becoming a bellwether for whether public markets still believe in AI valuations, and understanding net loss driven by accounting rather than operations is a genuinely useful skill for reading any growth company's financial filing.

Startups & funding

AMD pays roughly $2.9 billion for inference-chip startup Taalas

The deal deepens AMD's push into chips built specifically to run AI models cheaply, rather than train them.

AMD has acquired AI chip startup Taalas for approximately $2.9 billion, strengthening its line-up of processors built for inference, the stage where a trained AI model is actually used to generate answers, as distinct from the much more compute-intensive training stage. The deal adds Taalas's inference-focused chip designs to AMD's existing accelerator roadmap as it competes with Nvidia for a bigger share of the AI hardware market.

Training a large AI model is enormously expensive and happens relatively rarely, but running it to serve millions of users every day, inference, is where ongoing costs pile up. Specialised inference chips, often application-specific integrated circuits or ASICs, can be far cheaper to run for this purpose than general-purpose GPUs originally designed for training. By acquiring Taalas rather than building similar technology from scratch, AMD gains engineering talent and designs more quickly.

The acquisition fits a broader pattern of large chipmakers buying smaller specialists to fill gaps in their product line, rather than relying solely on internal research. For AMD, it's a bet that owning dedicated inference silicon will help it chip away at Nvidia's dominance as AI companies increasingly care more about running costs than raw training power.

Sources: BigGo Finance · OC3D · KuCoin

Key concepts in this story
AI inference chip (reminder)
A processor optimised for running an already-trained AI model efficiently and cheaply, rather than training it from scratch.
Strategic acquisition
Buying a smaller company primarily to obtain its technology, patents or engineering team quickly, rather than for its existing revenue or customer base.
ASIC (reminder)
A chip custom-built for one specific job, like running AI inference, which can be far more efficient than a general-purpose chip for that single task.
Why it matters for you

If you're interested in hardware or semiconductor investing, this shows deal-making is shifting from training-focused GPUs toward cheaper inference chips, a theme likely to keep driving M&A activity.

Startups & funding

Isomorphic Labs raises €4.5 billion to accelerate AI-designed drugs

The DeepMind spin-off, built around AlphaFold's protein-structure breakthroughs, lands one of Europe's largest AI funding rounds.

Isomorphic Labs, a company using AI to design new medicines, has raised €4.5 billion in a new funding round, one of the largest AI deals to come out of Europe this year. The company grew out of Google DeepMind, leveraging the AlphaFold technology that won its creators a Nobel Prize for predicting how proteins fold, a step that is central to understanding diseases and designing drugs to treat them.

Traditional drug discovery can take over a decade and cost billions of pounds per approved medicine, with most candidate compounds failing in testing. Isomorphic's pitch is that AI models can simulate how potential drug molecules will behave far faster than lab experiments alone, narrowing down candidates before expensive physical trials begin.

The scale of the round reflects investor enthusiasm for 'vertical AI', models built for a single high-value industry rather than general use, with drug discovery seen as one of the sectors where AI could have the most tangible real-world impact. Whether that promise translates into actual approved medicines, and real revenue, remains the key test ahead.

Sources: EU-Startups

Key concepts in this story
AI drug discovery
Using AI models to predict how molecules will behave in the body, helping researchers narrow down which drug candidates are worth testing in costly, time-consuming lab and clinical trials.
Spin-off company
A new, independent business created out of part of an existing company; Isomorphic Labs was spun out of Google DeepMind to focus specifically on medicine.
Vertical AI (reminder)
AI built and tuned for one specific industry, like drug discovery or law, rather than general-purpose use.
Why it matters for you

This is a strong example of AI investment moving beyond chatbots into capital-intensive scientific fields, relevant if you're considering biotech, pharma or deep-tech venture roles.

Code corner

Calculating Anthropic's eye-watering revenue growth

Based on: Anthropic set to price IPO above $70–78 range as demand surges

Anthropic's prospectus said revenue grew roughly 1,200% year-on-year. Let's write a small function to calculate growth rates like this ourselves, and use it to sanity-check the headline number from the article.

def growth_rate(old_value, new_value):
    """Return percentage growth from old_value to new_value."""
    if old_value == 0:
        raise ValueError("Cannot calculate growth from zero")
    change = new_value - old_value
    percent = (change / old_value) * 100
    return round(percent, 1)

# Figures from Anthropic's prospectus (nine months to September, in billions)
revenue_this_year = 33.24
# Implied prior-year revenue, based on the ~1200% growth reported
revenue_last_year = revenue_this_year / 13  # growth of 1200% means new = 13x old

rate = growth_rate(revenue_last_year, revenue_this_year)

print(f"Revenue last year (approx): ${revenue_last_year:.2f}bn")
print(f"Revenue this year: ${revenue_this_year:.2f}bn")
print(f"Growth rate: {rate}%")

if rate > 1000:
    print("That's extraordinary growth, even by AI industry standards.")
Line by line
  1. def growth_rate(old_value, new_value): defines a reusable function with two inputs, the earlier and later figures.
  2. The function raises an error if old_value is zero, since you can't calculate a percentage change from nothing — division by zero would crash the program otherwise.
  3. change = new_value - old_value finds the absolute increase in revenue, then percent = (change / old_value) * 100 converts that into a percentage.
  4. We work backwards from the reported 1,200% growth to estimate last year's revenue (new = 13 × old when growth is 1200%, since 1200% growth means the new figure is old + 12×old = 13×old).
  5. print(f"...") uses an f-string to neatly embed variable values inside text, rounding to a sensible number of decimal places.
  6. The final if statement shows how you can add simple conditional commentary based on a calculated result.

Try it: Change revenue_last_year to use the actual 2025 figure (around $2.55bn) instead of the estimate, and see how close the function's calculated growth rate comes to the reported 1,200%.
