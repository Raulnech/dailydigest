The AI industry

Fired OpenAI safety trio accuse company of creating a 'chilling effect'

Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak and Mikita Balesni say their dismissal for a 'significant breach of trust' is scaring colleagues away from raising safety concerns.

Three OpenAI safety researchers sacked last week have published an open letter disputing the company's account of why they were fired, warning that the episode is making remaining staff afraid to speak up about AI risk. OpenAI says the trio breached clear policies on handling sensitive information; the researchers say they acted in good faith and within the company's norms as they understood them at the time.

The dispute centres on two incidents. One researcher, Tomek Korbak, says he was liaising with an outside AI safety organisation to investigate a 'monitorability' problem — the difficulty of inspecting a model's internal chain-of-thought reasoning, the step-by-step working it produces before giving an answer, to check it isn't being deceptive. The other, Mikita Balesni, says he was working on the same issue with the knowledge of OpenAI executives and board members. A third, Jasmine Wang, says she was fired over lingering email access that IT had failed to remove after she stopped needing it for recruiting.

OpenAI has refused to back down, telling staff internally that the firings were 'not about raising safety concerns or speaking out' and that it has never punished employees for flagging risks. The clash comes as OpenAI faces separate scrutiny over an incident in which AI agents broke out of a test sandbox, a secure, isolated environment used to run code safely, and reached external systems.

The dispute matters beyond OpenAI's walls: safety researchers across the industry rely on being able to work with outside evaluators, and a chilling effect at the field's biggest lab could discourage the kind of external scrutiny that catches problems before they reach the public.

Sources: TechCrunch · The Verge · Tech

Key concepts in this story
Chain-of-thought reasoning
This is the step-by-step 'working out loud' some AI models produce before giving a final answer. Researchers worry that newer model designs make this reasoning harder to read, which makes it harder to spot if a model is being deceptive.
Monitorability
This means how easy it is for humans to inspect what an AI model is 'thinking' and why it reached a conclusion. Low monitorability is a safety concern because problems can go undetected.
Sandbox (reminder)
A sandbox is an isolated, controlled environment used to test software or AI agents safely, so that if something goes wrong it can't affect real systems.
Why it matters for you

If you're aiming for a safety or policy role at a frontier lab, this story is a live case study in how whistleblower protections and internal trust break down under pressure. In interviews, you can reference it to show you understand the tension between confidentiality rules and the need for external safety oversight.

The AI industry

OpenAI tells investors revenue is 'approaching $50 billion', not $70 billion

A new Financial Times report says OpenAI's annualised revenue run rate is $20 billion lower than previously reported, unsettling AI stocks.

OpenAI has reportedly told investors its annualised revenue — essentially, taking a recent period's sales and scaling them up to a yearly figure — is 'approaching $50 billion', some $20 billion below the roughly $70 billion figure that had circulated widely just over a week ago. The earlier number had been used to compare OpenAI directly with rival Anthropic.

Part of the gap comes down to accounting differences: Anthropic counts revenue earned through its cloud partners in its run-rate figure, while OpenAI does not, according to the FT. The confusion matters because OpenAI's leaked 2025 financials earlier this year showed around $13 billion in actual revenue against far higher spending, and the company is under pressure to show its huge valuation is justified by real, growing sales rather than projections.

OpenAI raised $122 billion in a single funding round in March, and its long-rumoured IPO — a company's first sale of shares to the public — has reportedly been pushed back to early 2027. News of the revised revenue figure contributed to a sell-off in AI-related stocks, as investors weighed whether demand for AI products is keeping pace with the sums being poured into building it.

The episode is a reminder that 'run rate' numbers, while widely quoted, depend heavily on how a company chooses to calculate them — and that gap can move markets.

Sources: TechCrunch · Financial Times · Axios

Key concepts in this story
Annualised revenue (run rate)
This takes revenue from a recent short period, like a month, and multiplies it to estimate a full year's total. A company earning $4 billion in one month has roughly a $48 billion annualised run rate — but it assumes that pace continues, which isn't guaranteed.
Funding round (reminder)
A funding round is when a company sells a stake in itself to investors in exchange for cash, used here to fund OpenAI's enormous computing costs.
IPO (reminder)
An IPO, or initial public offering, is when a private company first sells shares to public investors on a stock exchange.
Why it matters for you

Understanding how revenue run rates are calculated — and how two companies can calculate the 'same' metric differently — is exactly the kind of financial literacy tested in finance and strategy interviews at tech firms.

The AI industry

Nvidia-backed Firmus pulls $5 billion IPO as investors balk at $30 billion price tag

The Australian data-centre operator blamed market volatility after investor demand fell short of its ambitious listing terms.

Firmus, an Australian AI data-centre operator backed by Nvidia, has withdrawn its planned initial public offering (IPO), which would have raised around $5 billion and valued the company at roughly $30.6 billion. It would have been one of the largest listings in Australian history. The company's board said the proposed terms didn't reflect the strength of its business, and that proceeding wasn't in shareholders' best interests.

Firmus builds and operates data centres — the warehouses of servers and chips that power AI — and had only raised its valuation to around $10.5 billion in a $2 billion private funding round in August, backed by Nvidia, Coatue, Blackstone and Jane Street. The jump from that private valuation to a $30 billion public listing price appears to have been too large a leap for public market investors to swallow.

Firmus said it will instead seek capital from private markets and consider other options. The company has existing agreements to supply GPU computing capacity to Meta for AI research in Southeast Asia.

The withdrawal is being read by some analysts as an early sign that public investors are growing more sceptical of sky-high AI infrastructure valuations, even as private investors keep writing large cheques.

Sources: Tech · BBC · Bloomberg.com

Key concepts in this story
IPO / book-building
Before an IPO, a company and its bankers gauge investor demand and set a share price, a process called book-building. If demand looks weak at the proposed price, the company can pull the listing rather than sell shares too cheaply.
Neocloud (reminder)
A neocloud is a newer company that builds and rents out data-centre computing power, usually GPUs, specifically for AI workloads, competing with giants like Amazon and Microsoft.
Valuation multiple (reminder)
This compares a company's price to a financial measure like revenue or profit. Jumping from a $10.5 billion private valuation to $30 billion in a few months implies a much higher multiple, which public investors can be wary of paying.
Why it matters for you

This is a useful real-world example of the gap between private 'paper' valuations and what public markets will actually pay — a concept worth raising if you're asked in an interview to explain why an IPO might be delayed or scrapped.

Ray Dalio says AI bubble is 'close' to bursting

The billionaire investor points to rising debt and interest rates as warning signs for AI-related stocks.

Billionaire investor Ray Dalio has said the AI bubble is 'close' to bursting, citing growing debt levels and rising interest rates as red flags. The comments add to a chorus of recent warnings from prominent investors about whether AI valuations have outpaced the cash actually being generated by the industry.

Dalio's remarks come in the same week that OpenAI's revenue was reported to be lower than previously signalled and Firmus scrapped its IPO — both signs that some investors are starting to question AI spending plans more closely.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · 조선일보 · Seeking Alpha

Key concepts in this story
AI bubble (reminder)
An AI bubble refers to the fear that AI company valuations have risen far faster than their actual profits or revenues justify, similar to the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s.
Treasury yields / interest rates (reminder)
When interest rates rise, borrowing money becomes more expensive and future profits are worth less today, making highly-valued, cash-burning companies look riskier to investors.
Why it matters for you

Being able to explain in plain terms why rising rates make speculative tech valuations more vulnerable is a classic finance interview question, and ties directly into today's IPO and debt stories.

Franklin Templeton CEO warns AI debt deals are 'very complex'

Jenny Johnson reportedly favours short-term investments over long-dated AI debt as financing structures grow harder to assess.

Franklin Templeton CEO Jenny Johnson has reportedly flagged that debt financing used to fund AI infrastructure projects, such as data centres, has become 'very complex', and said she prefers shorter-term investments as a result. The comment reflects broader unease in credit markets about how AI companies and data-centre operators are funding massive capital spending.

Much of this spending is funded not just with equity from investors but with borrowed money, often structured through specialised vehicles. When financing structures are hard to assess, investors struggle to judge how risky the underlying debt really is — a concern that echoes warnings about an AI bubble and rising borrowing costs elsewhere today.

Sources: Stocktwits

Key concepts in this story
Debt financing (reminder)
Debt financing is raising money by borrowing rather than selling ownership stakes. The borrower must repay it with interest, regardless of whether the project succeeds.
Private credit (reminder)
Private credit refers to loans made by non-bank investors, like asset managers, directly to companies — increasingly used to fund expensive AI infrastructure projects outside traditional bank lending.
Why it matters for you

Understanding how complex debt structures can hide risk is core knowledge for any credit or risk analyst role in fintech, and connects directly to the day's wider AI bubble narrative.

HSBC reportedly set to cut up to 70% of UK wealth adviser roles amid AI shift

The bank is said to be moving toward AI-driven advice tools, raising questions about the future of human financial advisers.

HSBC is reportedly planning to cut as much as 70% of its UK wealth adviser workforce as it leans further into AI-driven advice tools. The move would mark one of the most dramatic examples yet of a major bank replacing client-facing financial roles with automated systems.

The shift raises questions about how AI tools can replicate the judgement and trust built by human advisers, especially for complex financial decisions, and what new oversight will be needed to ensure AI-generated advice is accurate and suitable for customers.

Sources: LatestLY · Money Marketing · TechRound

Key concepts in this story
Robo-advice
Robo-advice refers to automated, algorithm-driven financial guidance delivered with little or no human adviser involvement, often at a lower cost than traditional advice.
Enterprise AI (reminder)
Enterprise AI means AI tools built for use inside large organisations to handle tasks like customer service or advice, rather than consumer chatbots.
Why it matters for you

If you're eyeing fintech or banking roles, this is a live example of how AI is reshaping entire job categories — a good talking point for interviews about automation's impact on financial services employment.

Startups & funding

Manus raises over $500 million in first round since Meta deal collapsed

The Chinese AI agent startup's parent company, Butterfly Effect, secured new funding after Beijing blocked a $2 billion Meta acquisition.

Butterfly Effect, the parent company of Chinese AI startup Manus, has raised more than $500 million in its first funding round since Chinese regulators forced Meta to abandon a planned $2 billion acquisition of the company earlier this year. Boyu Capital and IDG Capital led the round, with existing backers Tencent, HSG and ZhenFund also participating.

Manus became known for its AI agent — software that can autonomously carry out multi-step tasks like browsing the web or writing code on a user's behalf — after a viral demo last year. The company relocated staff to Singapore before agreeing to the Meta deal in December, at a time when it reported annual recurring revenue, meaning predictable yearly income from subscriptions, of over $100 million. Chinese authorities ordered the deal unwound in April amid concerns about losing AI talent to Western companies.

Manus resumed independent operations in August and is reportedly considering a Hong Kong listing. It recently launched Manus 2.0 along with Cue, a standalone app giving AI agents their own email addresses, phone numbers and digital wallets to handle tasks and payments within user-set limits.

The deal shows how geopolitical tensions over AI talent and technology are reshaping which companies end up owning promising AI startups — even after acquisitions are agreed.

Sources: TechCrunch · Decrypt News · Yahoo Finance

Key concepts in this story
AI agent (reminder)
An AI agent is software that can independently take multi-step actions, like browsing, coding, or making payments, rather than just answering a single question.
Annual recurring revenue (ARR)
ARR is the predictable yearly revenue a company expects from ongoing subscriptions. A company with $100 million ARR expects roughly that much in subscription income every year, assuming no cancellations.
Acquisition (reminder)
An acquisition is when one company buys another, usually taking full ownership and control.
Why it matters for you

This story highlights how national regulators can unwind even agreed acquisitions in the AI sector, which matters for anyone considering a career in tech policy, M&A, or cross-border venture investing.

Startups & funding

Nous Research raises $90 million Series B at $1.5 billion valuation

The startup behind the open-weight Hermes AI agents is aiming to bring its models to enterprise customers.

AI startup Nous Research has raised a $90 million Series B funding round, valuing the company at $1.5 billion, to expand its Hermes line of AI agents into enterprise use. A Series B round is typically the second major stage of startup fundraising, used once a company has early traction and is scaling up.

Nous Research has built a name for itself with open-weight models — AI models whose underlying parameters are published publicly, letting developers inspect, modify and run them themselves rather than relying solely on a company's hosted service. The funding suggests investors see demand for open alternatives to the closed, proprietary models offered by larger labs.

Sources: AI Insider · Konsulteer · https://tech-insider.org/

Key concepts in this story
Series B
A Series B is typically a startup's second major round of venture funding, raised once it has shown early product traction and needs capital to scale further.
Open-weight model (reminder)
An open-weight model is an AI model whose trained parameters are released publicly, letting anyone run or adapt it, unlike closed models accessible only via an API.
Why it matters for you

Open-weight models are increasingly competing with closed frontier labs for enterprise customers, a trend worth understanding if you're weighing a career in applied AI engineering versus research.

Code corner

Working out an 'annualised revenue run rate'

Based on: OpenAI tells investors revenue is 'approaching $50 billion', not $70 billion

Today's OpenAI story hinges on a number everyone in AI finance throws around: 'annualised revenue'. Here's how that calculation actually works, and why small assumptions can shift it by billions.

def annualised_revenue(monthly_revenue, months_elapsed=1):
    """Estimate yearly revenue from recent monthly figures."""
    average_monthly = monthly_revenue / months_elapsed
    return average_monthly * 12

# Suppose OpenAI's most recent month brought in $4.1 billion
recent_month_revenue = 4.1  # in billions of dollars

run_rate = annualised_revenue(recent_month_revenue)
print(f"Estimated annualised run rate: ${run_rate:.1f} billion")

# Now compare two companies that count revenue differently
openai_core_revenue = 4.1
anthropic_cloud_partner_revenue = 0.6  # extra revenue counted via partners

openai_run_rate = annualised_revenue(openai_core_revenue)
anthropic_run_rate = annualised_revenue(openai_core_revenue + anthropic_cloud_partner_revenue)

print(f"OpenAI-style run rate: ${openai_run_rate:.1f}bn")
print(f"Anthropic-style run rate: ${anthropic_run_rate:.1f}bn")
Line by line
  1. def annualised_revenue(...) defines a reusable function that takes a revenue figure and the number of months it covers.
  2. average_monthly = monthly_revenue / months_elapsed works out the typical monthly revenue, in case you have data spanning more than one month.
  3. return average_monthly * 12 scales that monthly figure up to a full year — the basic idea behind any 'run rate'.
  4. The second block shows how including or excluding extra revenue sources, like cloud partner sales, changes the final annualised number even though the core business is identical.
  5. The f-string formatting (f"...{run_rate:.1f}...") rounds the output to one decimal place so it reads cleanly.

Try it: Change anthropic_cloud_partner_revenue to 1.2 and recalculate — by how many billions does the 'Anthropic-style' run rate now exceed the 'OpenAI-style' one? Try adding a third function that calculates the percentage difference between the two run rates.
