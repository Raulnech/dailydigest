The AI industry

OpenAI says ethical hackers used rival Anthropic's Claude to breach its systems

A small team of white-hat researchers reportedly exploited OpenAI's defences using Anthropic's Claude Opus 5, highlighting a new front in AI security testing.

OpenAI has confirmed that a small group of security researchers managed to breach its systems using Claude Opus 5, a large language model built by rival lab Anthropic, according to reports from the Financial Times, the Wall Street Journal, CoinGape and VentureBeat. The researchers are described as "white-hat" hackers, meaning they were testing for weaknesses with permission rather than trying to cause harm.

The case is notable because it shows one company's AI model being used as a tool to probe another company's security, rather than the more familiar scenario of a human hacker using code alone. As AI models become more capable at writing and adapting attack code, security teams across the industry are increasingly running these kinds of exercises, sometimes called red-teaming, to find weaknesses before real criminals do.

Details of exactly how the breach was carried out have not been fully disclosed in the reporting so far. The episode adds to a broader conversation this year about AI safety, coming just as Nvidia's chief executive and leaders from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind met UK officials to discuss the risks of advanced AI systems.

Sources: Financial Times · wsj.com · CoinGape

Key concepts in this story
White-hat hacking
White-hat hackers break into systems with permission, to find and fix flaws before criminals exploit them – think of a locksmith testing your front door lock rather than a burglar picking it.
Red-teaming
Red-teaming means deliberately attacking your own product to find weaknesses. AI labs increasingly red-team their models by trying to trick them into unsafe behaviour.
Cross-model attack
This is when one AI system is used as a tool to test or attack a different company's AI system, rather than a human writing all the attack code by hand.
Why it matters for you

This story is a preview of AI security becoming its own career path: firms will pay well for people who can red-team models and write up findings clearly. In an interview, you could mention that testing AI systems with other AI systems is now a recognised practice, not just theory.

The AI industry

Nvidia's Huang says chip sales will double again next year

Nvidia's chief executive told a UK summit that the company expects to sell twice as many chips in the next twelve months as it did this year.

Speaking at a summit in Scotland alongside King Charles III, Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang said he expects the company to sell double the number of chips next year compared with this year. It follows Nvidia's own forecast of 70% revenue growth for its fiscal year ending January 2028, equivalent to roughly $673 billion, a number that has already pushed analysts' expectations higher.

Nvidia doesn't publish exact chip volumes, but it makes far more than just its well-known data centre GPUs (graphics processing units, the chips that power most AI training and inference). Its product range also includes CPUs, networking chips, laptop chips, and even the processor inside Nintendo's Switch 2 console. Last year Huang said Nvidia shipped six million of its Blackwell GPUs in a single year, giving a sense of scale.

Huang's comments came during discussions on AI safety, a topic he has addressed repeatedly as public concern grows. He was joined by representatives from Google DeepMind, OpenAI and Anthropic. Huang argued that unsafe products should be held back and re-engineered, a notable statement from the boss of the company supplying the hardware behind almost every major AI system.

Sources: Tech · aa.com.tr

Key concepts in this story
GPU (graphics processing unit)
A GPU is a chip originally designed for video game graphics that turns out to be extremely good at the maths behind AI, because it can do thousands of calculations at once. Training and running large AI models depends heavily on GPUs.
Inference
Inference is when a trained AI model is actually used to answer a question or generate an output, as opposed to the earlier training phase where it learns from data.
Revenue forecast
A revenue forecast is a company's own prediction of how much money it will make in a future period, often used by investors to judge whether a stock is fairly priced.
Why it matters for you

Chip demand forecasts like this shape hiring across the AI supply chain, from data centre engineers to finance teams valuing chipmakers. If asked in an interview why AI stocks are volatile, you can point to how heavily markets react to a single Nvidia forecast.

The AI industry

Chinese AI models earn a tenth of OpenAI and Anthropic's combined revenue

Research group Rhodium found Chinese AI labs are growing fast in usage but lagging far behind on revenue, even as some carry sky-high valuations.

All of China's AI models combined generate only about 10% of the revenue reported by OpenAI and Anthropic, according to new estimates from US research firm Rhodium Group. The figures use annual recurring revenue (ARR), a common industry measure that takes a recent month's revenue and multiplies it by twelve to estimate a yearly run rate.

DeepSeek's ARR was the lowest among major Chinese firms at $500 million, followed by MiniMax at $800 million and Moonshot at $1 billion. Even adding in ByteDance's $4 billion and Alibaba's $2.4 billion, the total remains dwarfed by OpenAI's reported $40 billion and Anthropic's $65 billion. Yet some Chinese firms carry valuations far out of proportion to that revenue: Rhodium estimated Moonshot's valuation at 50 times revenue and DeepSeek's at 163 times, compared with 34 times for OpenAI and 21 times for Anthropic.

Rhodium's analysts said the financing gap will make it harder for Chinese frontier labs to scale sustainably, noting that over 60% of equity investment in Chinese AI chips and servers has come from state-affiliated sources. Anthropic is reportedly preparing a US listing next month, while OpenAI has pushed its IPO plans to next year, and Moonshot and DeepSeek are reportedly exploring listings of their own.

Sources: Tech · TradingView · PYMNTS.com

Key concepts in this story
Annual recurring revenue (ARR)
ARR estimates a company's yearly revenue by taking one recent month and multiplying by twelve. If a company earned $10 million last month, its ARR would be roughly $120 million – useful for fast-growing firms, but it can overstate reality if growth slows.
Revenue multiple
A revenue multiple compares a company's valuation to its revenue. A company valued at $1 billion with $10 million in revenue has a multiple of 100x – a sign investors expect huge future growth, or a sign of hype.
Open-weight model
Reminder: an open-weight model shares its trained parameters publicly so anyone can download and run it, unlike closed models such as GPT or Claude which only run on the company's own servers.
Why it matters for you

Understanding ARR and revenue multiples is core analyst-interview material for anyone eyeing fintech or venture roles, since these numbers are how AI companies get valued despite huge losses. It also links directly to today's CoreWeave and Emulate stories, where investors are betting on growth rather than current profit.

CoreWeave launches $3 billion bond sale to fund AI expansion

The cloud computing firm is raising convertible notes as its revenue and debt both climb sharply.

CoreWeave, a cloud computing company that rents out AI computing power, has launched a $3 billion sale of convertible senior notes, a type of bond that can later be converted into company shares. The move comes as the firm's revenue and debt load both continue to grow rapidly, according to reports from Digitimes, Crypto Briefing and The Economic Times.

Convertible notes let investors lend money to a company now, with the option to swap that debt for shares later, usually at a set price. They are popular with fast-growing but capital-hungry companies because they can often be issued at lower interest rates than ordinary bonds, since investors get the extra upside of potentially owning equity.

CoreWeave's stock reportedly fell more than 4% following the announcement, a common reaction when a company signals it needs to raise more debt to keep expanding. The raise underlines how capital-intensive the AI infrastructure business has become, with companies like CoreWeave racing to build out data centres to meet demand for AI computing.

Sources: digitimes · Crypto Briefing · The Economic Times

Key concepts in this story
Convertible senior notes
These are bonds that give the lender the right to convert their debt into company shares later. It's like lending someone money with the option to become a part-owner of their business instead of being repaid in cash.
Debt financing
Debt financing means raising money by borrowing rather than selling shares. The company must pay it back with interest, unlike equity financing where investors simply own a slice of the business.
Why it matters for you

Reading bond and debt announcements like this is a basic skill for finance and fintech analyst roles, since it tells you how a company plans to fund growth without diluting shareholders. It's worth linking to the Meta/CleanSpark story below, which shows a similar debt-fuelled build-out pattern across the AI infrastructure sector.

CleanSpark seeks $2.2 billion to build Meta-linked AI data centre

CleanSpark disclosed Meta as the tenant behind a 20-year data centre lease and is raising billions through a junk-bond sale to fund construction.

CleanSpark, a company better known for cryptocurrency mining infrastructure, has revealed that Meta is the tenant behind a newly signed 20-year lease for an AI data centre in Georgia. To fund the buildout, a CleanSpark unit is reportedly seeking to raise more than $2.2 billion through a junk-bond offering, according to Stock Titan, TradingView, Crypto Briefing and The Deep Dive.

A junk bond is a bond issued by a company considered to have a higher risk of failing to repay its debt, and so investors demand higher interest rates to compensate. The lease itself is reportedly valued at around $6.6 billion over its full term, showing how much capital is now flowing into physical infrastructure to support AI computing demand.

The arrangement illustrates a growing pattern: major tech firms like Meta are increasingly leasing data centre capacity from specialist infrastructure companies rather than building everything themselves, while those infrastructure firms take on significant debt to finance construction.

Sources: Stock Titan · TradingView · Crypto Briefing

Key concepts in this story
Junk bond
A junk bond is debt issued by a company seen as riskier, so it pays a higher interest rate to attract lenders. It's the financial equivalent of a landlord charging a risky tenant a higher deposit.
Data centre lease
A data centre lease is a long-term rental agreement for computing infrastructure – buildings full of servers and chips – rather than a company owning and building it outright.
Why it matters for you

This shows how AI's physical infrastructure boom is being financed through debt markets, a theme worth raising if asked about risks in the AI sector during an interview. It pairs neatly with CoreWeave's story above as another example of AI firms leaning on borrowed money to expand.

SEC opens legal pathway for tokenised US stock trading

A new Innovation Exemption lets certain platforms issue blockchain-based versions of publicly traded shares, potentially paving the way towards round-the-clock trading.

The US Securities and Exchange Commission issued an order on Thursday creating a regulatory pathway, called the Innovation Exemption, allowing certain trading venues to offer tokenised versions of publicly traded US stocks. Tokenisation means creating a digital representation of an asset, such as a share, on a blockchain, a shared digital ledger that records transactions.

The exemption requires that token holders keep the same rights as traditional shareholders, including voting rights and dividends, and gives companies the right to object before their shares are tokenised. It follows the Senate's failure this week to advance the Clarity Act, a bill meant to set clear rules for crypto markets, meaning the SEC is now defining the boundary itself through existing powers.

SEC chair Paul Atkins said the five-year exemption is meant to let the market develop while informing future permanent rules. Platforms including Coinbase, Robinhood, Gemini and Kraken have already launched tokenised stock products overseas but not yet in the US. Supporters say tokenisation could eventually enable 24/7 trading and easier integration with blockchain finance, though the SEC has added volume limits to guard against the extra volatility that thinner, round-the-clock markets can bring.

Sources: Tech · Yahoo Finance · Crypto Briefing

Key concepts in this story
Tokenisation
Tokenisation means creating a digital token on a blockchain that represents ownership of a real asset, like a share of Apple stock, so it can be traded using crypto-style infrastructure.
Blockchain
A blockchain is a shared, tamper-resistant digital ledger that many computers keep copies of, so no single party controls the record of who owns what.
24/7 trading
Traditional stock markets only trade during set weekday hours; 24/7 trading would let investors buy and sell at any time, similar to how cryptocurrencies already trade.
Why it matters for you

Fintech roles increasingly sit at the intersection of traditional markets and crypto infrastructure, so understanding tokenisation is useful shorthand in interviews at banks or crypto-adjacent startups. It also connects to today's AI infrastructure financing stories, since tokenised assets could eventually be used as new forms of collateral or investment vehicles for AI-related debt.

Startups & funding

Emulate seeks $700 million seed round at up to $4 billion valuation

A UK startup founded by the creators of DeepMind's Genie world model is reportedly raising a huge seed round to build simulations for robotics.

Emulate, a UK startup founded by former creators of DeepMind's Genie project, is reportedly seeking to raise $700 million in a seed round, an unusually large sum for a company only weeks old, according to Tech Funding News, PYMNTS and Crypto Briefing. Reports suggest the valuation being discussed is approaching $4 billion, up from an initial $3 billion figure mentioned in some coverage.

The company is reportedly building "world models", AI systems trained to simulate how the physical world behaves, which can be used to train robots or test scenarios virtually before deploying them in reality. This follows a broader industry push to use AI simulation as a cheaper, faster alternative to physical trial and error in robotics.

Details beyond the reported fundraising figures remain limited, and the numbers should be treated as unconfirmed until Emulate or its investors comment directly.

Sources: Tech Funding News · PYMNTS.com · جولة

Key concepts in this story
World model
A world model is an AI system trained to predict how an environment will change over time, effectively simulating physics and cause-and-effect so robots or agents can practice in a virtual world before acting in the real one.
Seed round
A seed round is typically the first serious chunk of funding a startup raises, though this one is unusually large; it's meant to fund early growth before further, later funding rounds.
Private valuation
Reminder: a private valuation is what investors say a company is worth before it lists publicly, based on a recent funding round rather than a public share price.
Why it matters for you

Massive seed rounds like this show investors are betting heavily on robotics and simulation as the next big AI market, a useful trend to mention if discussing where AI investment is heading next. It also demonstrates how quickly private valuations can inflate before any real revenue exists, echoing the Chinese AI valuation story above.

Code corner

Comparing valuation multiples like an analyst

Based on: Chinese AI models earn a tenth of OpenAI and Anthropic's combined revenue

Today's story on Chinese AI models compared company valuations to their annual recurring revenue (ARR) using a 'revenue multiple'. Here's a simple Python script that does the same calculation for a small set of made-up companies, so you can see how a huge multiple stands out.

companies = {
    "OpenAI": {"arr_billion": 40, "valuation_billion": 1360},
    "Anthropic": {"arr_billion": 65, "valuation_billion": 1365},
    "Moonshot": {"arr_billion": 1, "valuation_billion": 50},
    "DeepSeek": {"arr_billion": 0.5, "valuation_billion": 81.5},
}

for name, data in companies.items():
    arr = data["arr_billion"]
    valuation = data["valuation_billion"]
    multiple = valuation / arr
    print(f"{name}: ARR ${arr}bn, valuation ${valuation}bn, multiple {multiple:.1f}x")

highest = max(companies, key=lambda n: companies[n]["valuation_billion"] / companies[n]["arr_billion"])
print(f"\nHighest multiple: {highest}")
Line by line
  1. `companies = {...}` creates a dictionary where each company name maps to another dictionary holding its ARR and valuation, both in billions of dollars.
  2. `for name, data in companies.items():` loops through every company, giving you its name and its inner dictionary of numbers on each pass.
  3. `multiple = valuation / arr` calculates the revenue multiple, exactly like Rhodium did for OpenAI, Anthropic, Moonshot and DeepSeek.
  4. The f-string (`f"..."`) formats the multiple to one decimal place using `:.1f`, so it prints cleanly like '34.0x' instead of a long decimal.
  5. `max(companies, key=lambda n: ...)` finds whichever company has the highest multiple by comparing that calculated ratio for every entry.

Try it: Add a new company to the dictionary with its own ARR and valuation, then rerun the script to see how its multiple compares to the others. Try making its valuation very high relative to its ARR and see how the 'Highest multiple' line changes.
