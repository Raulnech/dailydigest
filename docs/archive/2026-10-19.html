The AI industry

Anthropic Commits 5% of Shares to New 'Public Benefit Fund'

The AI lab says the vehicle will bankroll projects on AI's societal fallout, including job disruption.

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude chatbot, has said it will transfer 5% of its shares into a newly created 'public benefit fund', giving it a built-in pot of money to spend on projects it judges to be in society's interest. The fund will sit as a wholly-owned entity inside Anthropic, and the company can top it up with a further 2% of shares every time its overall valuation rises by 50%.

The move lands as the wider AI industry faces growing unease about job losses, misinformation and the concentration of power in a handful of companies. Anthropic has already put $200 million behind research into how AI might reshape the labour market, and the new fund is pitched as a way to go further, potentially funding anything from grants to traditional charities to direct payments to people affected by the technology. Chief executive Dario Amodei has described public attitudes to AI as 'fundamentally a crisis of trust', and the fund looks like an attempt to get ahead of that scepticism.

Exactly how the money will be spent, and who gets to decide, is still unclear: Anthropic says details on governance will follow in the coming months. For now, the announcement is as much a signal of intent as a concrete programme, a bet that visible goodwill on AI's downsides will help the company keep influence as rules for the industry are written.

Sources: TechCrunch · BBC · Al Jazeera

Key concepts in this story
Public benefit fund
A pot of money, in this case made of company shares, set aside specifically to pay for projects that benefit society rather than shareholders directly, similar to a corporate charitable foundation.
Equity stake
Owning a percentage of a company's shares means owning that percentage of its value; if Anthropic were worth £100bn, 5% in shares would be worth roughly £5bn.
Valuation-linked trigger
A rule that automatically increases a commitment when a metric crosses a threshold, here adding 2% more shares every time Anthropic's valuation grows by half.
Why it matters for you

This creates demand for people who can bridge AI policy, communications and corporate governance, roles increasingly asked about in interviews at AI labs. It is also a useful talking point if asked how AI companies are responding to fears about job disruption.

Microsoft Walks Away From Nscale's Planned UK AI Data Centre

Power supply concerns in Essex reportedly killed what would have been Britain's largest AI data centre project.

Microsoft has pulled out of a deal to anchor what would have been the UK's largest AI data centre, a project being built by Nscale in Essex, reportedly over concerns about the local electricity supply.

Data centres that train and run AI models need huge, reliable amounts of power, and connecting a new site to Britain's electricity grid can take years because of capacity constraints. Nscale is one of a new wave of 'neocloud' firms, specialist providers that rent out AI computing capacity rather than running consumer-facing cloud services, and the Microsoft deal had been central to its growth story.

The withdrawal comes as Nscale is trying to drum up investor support for a stock market listing, and it raises fresh questions about whether Britain's grid can keep pace with government ambitions to turn the country into an AI hub.

Sources: City AM · Telecompaper · w.media

Key concepts in this story
Data centre
A large building full of computer servers that store data and run software, including the huge clusters of chips needed to train and operate AI models.
Grid connection constraint
Before a new data centre can draw power, it often needs a formal connection to the national electricity grid, and demand for these connections can outstrip the grid's available capacity for years.
Neocloud
A reminder: a neocloud is a company that specialises in renting out AI computing power, rather than offering broad cloud services like storage or email.
Why it matters for you

Energy and grid-connection expertise is becoming as valuable to AI infrastructure firms as chip knowledge, a good angle if you're asked about bottlenecks in AI growth. It also links directly to today's Nscale IPO story below.

Santander Says Nvidia Chips Cut Its AI Running Costs by 99%

The bank is extending its Nvidia partnership after claiming specialised hardware made generating AI responses far cheaper.

Santander has renewed and expanded its partnership with Nvidia, saying the chipmaker's specialised AI infrastructure has cut the cost of generating AI responses inside the bank by 99%.

Running large language models is expensive: every question a customer or employee asks an AI assistant burns computing power, a cost known as inference. By using Nvidia's purpose-built hardware and software instead of general-purpose chips, Santander says it can run the same AI workloads far more cheaply, under a newly agreed three-year extension of the alliance.

For banks, inference costs are the hidden bill behind flashy AI chatbots and assistants, and shaving that bill can decide whether AI tools are profitable or a drain on the budget once they are rolled out to millions of customers.

Sources: PYMNTS.com · FF News · Finimize

Key concepts in this story
Inference
A reminder: inference is the process of an AI model actually generating an answer once it has been trained, and it costs computing power every single time it happens.
Specialised AI chip (ASIC)
A reminder: chips built specifically for AI workloads can run those tasks far more efficiently than general-purpose processors, cutting electricity and hardware costs.
Total cost of ownership
The full cost of running a system over time, not just the upfront price, including electricity, hardware and maintenance; cutting inference costs lowers this total bill.
Why it matters for you

Understanding cost-per-query economics is a practical talking point for interviews in AI infrastructure or fintech operations roles. It also shows why banks care as much about chip efficiency as about the AI models themselves.

Investors Push Back on Nscale's $30bn IPO Valuation

Microsoft's exit from its flagship UK data centre deal has reportedly spooked backers of Nscale's planned listing.

Investors in Nscale, the AI cloud computing firm at the centre of Microsoft's cancelled UK data centre deal, are reportedly pushing back on the roughly $30bn valuation the company is targeting for its initial public offering.

An IPO, or initial public offering, is when a private company sells shares to the public for the first time, turning a small circle of early investors into a listed stock anyone can buy. Nscale had positioned itself as a leading neocloud, but Microsoft's exit from its flagship UK project has reportedly made some investors nervous about whether the firm's growth story, and the price multiple it is asking for, still holds up.

With Nscale said to be seeking around $3bn from the float, the outcome will be watched closely as a test of investor appetite for AI infrastructure firms, after a year of soaring valuations across the sector.

Sources: Bloomberg.com · Demokratis.co.id · PYMNTS.com

Key concepts in this story
IPO (initial public offering)
The first time a private company sells shares on a public stock exchange, allowing ordinary investors to buy in and early backers to cash out some of their stake.
Valuation multiple
A reminder: this compares a company's price to a financial measure like revenue or profit, so a higher multiple means investors are paying more for each pound of earnings or sales.
Why it matters for you

This is a classic scenario for finance analysts: judging whether a hot sector's valuation is justified by fundamentals. It's a good example to bring up if asked in an interview how to assess an AI infrastructure IPO.

UK Regulator Tells AI Firms to Flag Limits of Financial Advice

The FCA wants chatbots to make clear when they are giving information rather than regulated advice.

The UK's Financial Conduct Authority has called on AI providers to be clearer about the limits of chatbots that answer financial questions, warning users that AI-generated output is not the same as regulated financial advice.

Financial advice in Britain is tightly regulated: firms must be authorised, meet standards, and can be held liable if they get it wrong, whereas general financial 'information' carries no such protection. The regulator is reportedly concerned that people are treating AI chatbot answers as reliable, personalised advice without realising there is no safety net if the answer turns out to be wrong.

The FCA has also set out a vision for 'agentic' finance, where AI systems can act on a customer's behalf, such as moving money or applying for products, but only within a framework that keeps a human accountable for the outcome.

Sources: Norton Rose Fulbright · Money Marketing · FF News

Key concepts in this story
Financial Conduct Authority (FCA)
The UK regulator that oversees banks, insurers and investment firms, setting rules to protect consumers and keep markets fair.
Regulated advice vs. information
Regulated advice is a personalised recommendation from an authorised adviser who can be held liable if it's wrong; general information, including a chatbot's reply, carries no such legal protection.
AI agent
A reminder: an AI agent is software that can take actions on a user's behalf, such as moving money, rather than just answering questions.
Why it matters for you

Compliance and fintech product roles increasingly need people who understand where AI tools cross from 'information' into regulated 'advice'. It's a strong point to raise if asked how a bank should deploy a customer-facing chatbot responsibly.

Five South Korean Bank Chiefs Apologise Over AI-Linked Hacking Incident

Bank bosses were grilled by lawmakers after a cyberattack reportedly tied to AI systems.

The chief executives of five of South Korea's biggest banks reportedly apologised before a parliamentary committee after a hacking incident linked to AI systems, promising to strengthen their cybersecurity.

The bank bosses were questioned during the National Assembly's annual audit of financial institutions, a formal process in which lawmakers can summon executives to account for failures. Full details of how AI was involved in the breach were not disclosed, but the chiefs reportedly committed to upgrading security to prevent a repeat.

The episode underlines a growing worry as banks adopt AI quickly: systems designed to speed up services can also open new doors for attackers, and regulators are increasingly willing to haul leadership in front of parliament when something goes wrong.

Sources: The Korea Times · 조선일보 · Chosunbiz

Key concepts in this story
Parliamentary audit
A formal review where lawmakers question executives of major institutions about their performance and failures, with the power to demand answers in public.
Data breach
An incident where sensitive information is accessed or stolen without authorisation, which can expose customers to fraud and cause lasting reputational damage.
Insider threat
A reminder: risk that comes from people with legitimate access to systems, rather than outside hackers, which becomes more complex once AI tools have broad internal access too.
Why it matters for you

This is a reminder that cybersecurity and risk roles in banking now need AI literacy, since AI systems can be both a target and a vector for attacks. Expect interviewers at banks to ask how you'd balance AI adoption with security oversight.

Startups & funding

Stripe to Buy AI Model Router OpenRouter for a Reported $7.5bn

The payments giant wants to sit at the centre of how businesses choose and pay for AI models.

Stripe, the payments company, has reportedly agreed to buy OpenRouter, a platform that routes requests between dozens of different AI models, in a deal said to be worth $7.5bn.

OpenRouter acts as a kind of traffic controller for AI: a developer sends it a request, and it decides which underlying model, from providers such as OpenAI, Anthropic or Google, should handle it, based on cost, speed or capability. For Stripe, which already sits in the middle of countless online payments, owning that routing layer would put it at the centre of a growing slice of AI spending too.

If confirmed, the deal would be one of the largest acquisitions of an AI infrastructure startup this year, giving Stripe a direct stake in how businesses choose and pay for AI models, rather than just processing the payments behind them.

Sources: Startup Fortune · Startup Fortune · Startup Fortune

Key concepts in this story
LLM router
A reminder: a system that sends each request to whichever AI model is cheapest, fastest or best suited, instead of relying on just one provider.
Aggregator business model
A company that sits between many suppliers and many customers, earning a cut by making it easier to choose between options, much like a price-comparison website for AI models.
Acquisition
A reminder: when one company buys another outright, usually to gain its technology, customers or talent rather than building the same thing from scratch.
Why it matters for you

This shows how payments and AI infrastructure are merging, creating demand for engineers and product managers who understand both billing systems and model routing. It directly sets up today's code corner, which simulates how a router like OpenRouter picks a model.

Anthropic Acquires Confidential Computing Startup Opaque Systems

The deal aims to let AI process sensitive data without exposing it, even to Anthropic itself.

Anthropic has reportedly acquired Opaque Systems, a startup specialising in 'confidential computing' technology that lets AI models process sensitive data without exposing it, even to the company running the servers.

Confidential computing uses secure hardware to keep data encrypted while it is actually being used in a calculation, not just when it is stored or sent over the internet. That matters for banks, hospitals or governments that want to use AI on sensitive records without that data ever being visible to outsiders, including the AI provider itself.

The acquisition suggests Anthropic wants to court security-conscious enterprise and government customers who have been hesitant to send confidential data to external AI models, an area that has become one of the most competitive battlegrounds among frontier AI labs.

Sources: SecurityWeek · Softonic

Key concepts in this story
Confidential computing
A way of processing data that keeps it encrypted even while a computer is actively working on it, so not even the operator of the server can see the raw information.
Encryption in use
Most encryption protects data at rest or in transit; encryption in use protects it during the actual calculation, closing one of the last gaps attackers could exploit.
Acquisition
A reminder: buying a smaller company outright, here to gain Opaque's technology and team rather than its existing customer base.
Why it matters for you

Security-focused AI engineering is becoming a specialist and well-paid niche, worth mentioning if you're asked about enterprise trust in AI. It also connects to the FCA and Korean bank stories today, all pointing to security as the next big constraint on AI adoption in finance.

Code corner

Building a Tiny AI Model Router

Based on: Stripe to Buy AI Model Router OpenRouter for a Reported $7.5bn

Stripe's reported purchase of OpenRouter is all about choosing the cheapest or best AI model for a given request. Here's a simplified Python version of that idea, using a fake list of models instead of a real API call.

import json

models = json.loads('''
[
  {"name": "fast-mini", "cost_per_1k_tokens": 0.002},
  {"name": "balanced-7b", "cost_per_1k_tokens": 0.008},
  {"name": "frontier-70b", "cost_per_1k_tokens": 0.03}
]
''')

def estimate_cost(model, prompt_tokens, completion_tokens):
    total_tokens = prompt_tokens + completion_tokens
    return (total_tokens / 1000) * model["cost_per_1k_tokens"]

prompt_tokens = 500
completion_tokens = 300

cheapest = min(models, key=lambda m: estimate_cost(m, prompt_tokens, completion_tokens))

for m in models:
    cost = estimate_cost(m, prompt_tokens, completion_tokens)
    print(f"{m['name']}: £{cost:.4f}")

print(f"Router picks: {cheapest['name']}")
Line by line
  1. json.loads(...) turns a text block into a Python list of dictionaries, simulating what a real API response from OpenRouter might look like.
  2. Each model dictionary has a name and a cost_per_1k_tokens figure, mirroring how AI providers typically charge per 1,000 tokens rather than per request.
  3. estimate_cost() adds the prompt tokens (what you send in) and completion tokens (what the model sends back) and scales the price accordingly, since both count towards the bill.
  4. min(models, key=...) is Python's way of finding the cheapest option by comparing the cost function's result for every model in the list, exactly what a router like OpenRouter does automatically.
  5. The final loop prints the cost for every model, then reveals which one the router would actually choose for this particular request.

Try it: Add a fourth model to the list with its own cost_per_1k_tokens, then change completion_tokens to 5000 to simulate a much longer answer, and see whether the router's choice changes.
