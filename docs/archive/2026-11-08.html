US Memo Claims Chinese Spy Had 'Close' Ties to Nvidia's Jensen Huang

Intelligence officials reportedly believe a Singapore-based executive was recruited by Chinese intelligence to get close to the world's most powerful chip boss.

A memo that circulated among US officials earlier this year claims that China's Ministry of State Security recruited technology executive Alice Huang, who works at Singapore-based firm Megaspeed, to spy on Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, according to reports from the New York Times and Financial Times. The Financial Times additionally reported the memo's claim that the pair had a romantic relationship.

The reports land amid intense scrutiny of Nvidia's sales of advanced chips to China. Nvidia's hardware, especially chips like the H200, is considered a dual-use technology: products with both civilian and military uses, meaning their export is tightly controlled by the US government to stop them ending up in weapons programmes or being used to train rival military AI systems. A separate New York Times investigation looked at New Matrix, a Chinese buyer authorised to purchase advanced Nvidia chips, and found its registered office was essentially empty, raising suspicions it might be a front company shielding a blacklisted firm.

Nvidia has pushed back, with a spokesperson telling the New York Times that Jensen Huang's contact with Alice Huang was 'not unusual' and that the company never shares classified information with customers. The spokesperson added that Nvidia sells 'the first, best and most' of its technology within the US. The story nonetheless adds to pressure on regulators weighing further restrictions on chip sales to China.

Sources: TechCrunch · PCMag UK · Security Boulevard

Key concepts in this story
Dual-use technology
A reminder: this is equipment or software that can serve both peaceful and military purposes, like a chip powerful enough to run both a hospital's AI diagnostics and a weapons-targeting system. Because of this, governments often restrict who can buy it.
Export controls
A reminder: these are government rules restricting the sale of sensitive technology, like advanced chips, to certain countries or buyers to protect national security.
Front company
A shell business set up to disguise who is really behind a purchase or transaction, often used to get around sanctions or export bans. An empty office with no real staff, as reporters found at New Matrix, is a classic warning sign.
Why it matters for you

If you're aiming for a role in compliance, cybersecurity, or trade policy, stories like this show why export control expertise is increasingly valuable to chipmakers. It's also a good interview talking point: it links geopolitics directly to a company's revenue, since China was once a huge market for Nvidia.

The AI industry

Microsoft Reportedly Pulls Back From Oracle Cloud Deal Over Ageing Nvidia Chips

Microsoft is said to be winding down an agreement to rent AI computing capacity from Oracle after deciding the hardware on offer was out of date.

Microsoft is reportedly winding down a deal under which it rented AI computing capacity from Oracle, according to multiple outlets. The reason cited is that the Nvidia chips Oracle was offering were older-generation hardware rather than the latest models that Microsoft needs to train and run its most advanced AI systems.

Deals like this are common in the industry: so-called neoclouds, companies that specialise in renting out banks of GPUs rather than running a full cloud platform, let giants like Microsoft add extra computing power quickly without building new data centres themselves. GPUs, or graphics processing units, are chips built to handle many calculations at once, which makes them far better suited to AI workloads than traditional CPUs.

If accurate, the move raises questions about Oracle's ability to keep pace with the fastest-moving part of the AI hardware market, and about how much spare capacity it can now resell elsewhere. It also underscores how quickly AI chip generations are ageing out of usefulness for cutting-edge model training.

Sources: The Daily Star · 24/7 Wall St. · Crowdfund Insider

Key concepts in this story
Neocloud
A reminder: a neocloud is a company that rents out large amounts of GPU computing power to other businesses, rather than offering a full suite of cloud services like storage and databases.
GPU vs CPU
A reminder: a GPU (graphics processing unit) can run thousands of simple calculations simultaneously, which suits AI training, while a CPU (central processing unit) handles tasks one after another and is better for general computing.
Compute capacity
This simply means the amount of computing power, measured in chips or processing time, that a company has available to train or run AI models. More capacity means more models can be trained faster.
Why it matters for you

This matters if you're tracking which cloud providers are winning or losing AI infrastructure business, a key skill for equity analysts covering tech. It's also a reminder that hardware generations in AI move fast, so being 'behind' by even a year can cost a company major contracts.

Big Tech's 'Hidden' AI Debt Reaches $3.2 Trillion, Report Finds

An analysis of five major US tech firms finds borrowing linked to AI infrastructure build-outs now equals nearly 80% of their total assets.

A report from BigGo Finance estimates that hidden AI-related debt across five major US technology companies has reached $3.2 trillion, close to 80% of their combined total assets. The figure reportedly includes borrowing structures that don't always show up clearly on a company's main balance sheet.

The scale of spending reflects the enormous capital expenditure, or capex, tech giants are pouring into data centres, chips and power infrastructure to compete in AI. Because some of this financing is arranged through leasing structures or joint ventures rather than straightforward loans, it can be harder for investors to see the true scale of a company's obligations.

The report adds to a growing debate about whether the AI spending boom is being financed sustainably, and whether companies have enough free cash flow to service this debt if AI revenue growth slows.

Sources: BigGo Finance

Key concepts in this story
Off-balance-sheet debt
A reminder: this is borrowing structured so it doesn't appear directly as a loan on a company's main balance sheet, for example through leasing deals or joint ventures, making total debt levels harder to spot at a glance.
Capital expenditure (capex)
A reminder: this is money a company spends on long-term physical assets like buildings, chips, or data centres, as opposed to day-to-day running costs.
Debt-to-asset ratio
This compares how much a company owes to how much it owns, calculated as total debt divided by total assets. A company with $80 of debt for every $100 of assets is carrying a much heavier debt load than one with $20.
Why it matters for you

Understanding hidden leverage is core to credit analysis and risk management roles in banking, and it's a sharp question to raise in an interview: 'how exposed is this company if AI demand disappoints?' It also connects to today's bond market stories, since heavier debt loads usually mean higher borrowing costs.

Anthropic's IPO Valuation Talk Slides as Low as $1.1 Trillion Amid AI Stock Rout

Reports diverge on the exact figure, but all point to Anthropic's targeted stock market debut valuation falling sharply as investors turn cautious on AI.

Reported estimates of Anthropic's planned initial public offering (IPO) valuation have fallen in recent days, with figures from different outlets ranging from $1.1 trillion to $1.5 trillion, down from earlier talk of around $1.8 trillion. The drops reportedly track a broader sell-off in AI-related stocks.

An IPO is the process by which a private company first sells shares to the public on a stock exchange, and the valuation set beforehand determines how much investors are willing to pay for those shares. Anthropic, maker of the Claude chatbot, is one of the leading frontier AI labs, the small group of companies building the most advanced general-purpose AI models.

Despite the lower numbers, Anthropic is reportedly pressing ahead with plans for a listing before the US Thanksgiving holiday. The swinging estimates highlight growing nervousness among investors about whether AI company valuations, many set during last year's funding boom, can be justified by actual revenue.

Sources: Forbes · KuCoin · TradingView

Key concepts in this story
IPO (initial public offering)
A reminder: this is when a private company sells shares to the public for the first time on a stock exchange, letting ordinary investors buy a stake in it.
Valuation
A reminder: this is an estimate of what a company is worth overall, often calculated by multiplying its share price by the total number of shares, or agreed privately between investors before any shares are issued.
AI bubble
A reminder: this refers to concern that AI company valuations have risen far beyond what their actual profits or revenues justify, similar to the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, and could fall sharply if sentiment shifts.
Why it matters for you

Valuation swings like this are a prime topic for finance interviews: you should be able to explain why investor sentiment, not just company fundamentals, can move a valuation by hundreds of billions of dollars in weeks. It also shows why timing an IPO is a high-stakes decision for any company's leadership.

Deutsche Bank Secures Lighter Capital Rules for 2027 as It Pushes Its AI Narrative

The German lender has reportedly won regulatory relief on capital requirements just as it tries to convince investors its AI investments are paying off.

Deutsche Bank has reportedly been granted a lighter capital requirement for 2027, reducing the amount of its own money it must hold in reserve against potential losses, according to reports. The news comes as the bank simultaneously tries to build a stronger story around its AI investments to reassure investors.

Capital requirements are rules set by banking regulators forcing lenders to keep a cushion of their own funds, rather than relying purely on customer deposits or borrowed money, so they can absorb losses without collapsing. A lighter requirement generally frees up more capital for a bank to lend, invest, or return to shareholders.

The reports suggest Deutsche Bank sees its AI narrative as central to how investors judge its future earnings, alongside its traditional lending business. How convincingly the bank can show AI is boosting its net profit will likely shape how markets react to its next results.

Sources: ad-hoc-news.de · ad-hoc-news.de

Key concepts in this story
Capital requirements
These are rules, set by bank regulators, requiring banks to hold a certain amount of their own money in reserve relative to their loans and investments, so they can survive losses without needing a bailout.
Regulator
A reminder: this is a government body overseeing an industry, such as banking, to enforce rules that protect consumers, investors, or the wider financial system.
Net profit
A reminder: this is what a company earns after subtracting all costs, taxes, and expenses from its revenue. It's the 'bottom line' figure investors watch most closely.
Why it matters for you

This story matters for anyone eyeing banking or regulatory roles, since capital rules directly affect how much banks can lend or invest, including in AI. It also shows how traditional banks, not just tech firms, are now being judged partly on their AI strategy.

Meta's Latest Bond Sale Signals a Costlier Era for Tech Debt

Investors reportedly demanded sharper terms on Meta's newest bond offering, a sign that borrowing to fund AI data centres is getting pricier.

Meta's latest bond sale, used to raise money to fund its AI infrastructure build-out, has reportedly shown signs that investors are demanding better terms than in the past. A bond is essentially an IOU: the company borrows money from investors and promises to pay it back with interest over a set period.

The interest rate a company pays on new bonds is heavily influenced by its credit spread, the extra interest investors demand above a safe benchmark rate to compensate for perceived risk. A wider spread signals investors see more risk in lending to that company, often pushing up the cost of future borrowing.

The report suggests that as tech giants take on ever-larger debt loads to pay for AI data centres and chips, the market may be starting to price in more caution about how sustainable that spending is, even for well-established companies like Meta.

Sources: Yahoo Finance UK

Key concepts in this story
Bond
A reminder: this is a loan an investor makes to a company or government, which promises to pay the money back later with interest, typically over a period of years.
Credit spread
A reminder: this is the extra interest rate a borrower pays above a safe benchmark, reflecting how risky investors think it is to lend to them. A wider spread means investors want more compensation for the risk.
Capital expenditure (capex)
A reminder: this is money spent on long-term assets like data centres and chips, as opposed to everyday operating costs.
Why it matters for you

Bond market reactions are a key early-warning signal that finance professionals watch closely, often before stock prices move. Being able to explain why investors might demand a higher interest rate from a profitable company like Meta shows genuine understanding of credit risk, a useful point in any banking interview.

Alphabet Builds $548 Million Stake in Qualcomm, Boosting Chipmaker's Data Centre Ambitions

Google's parent has quietly taken an equity position in Qualcomm, a vote of confidence as the chipmaker pushes into AI data centre hardware.

Alphabet, Google's parent company, has reportedly built a $548 million equity stake in Qualcomm, according to BigGo Finance. The investment is seen as a tailwind, or helpful boost, for Qualcomm as it expands beyond smartphone chips into hardware for AI data centres.

Taking an equity stake means buying shares in a company, giving the buyer a direct financial interest in how that company's share price performs. If Qualcomm's stock rises, Alphabet's stake becomes more valuable; if it falls, Alphabet records a loss on paper, even without selling the shares.

Qualcomm has traditionally been best known for chips used in mobile phones, but is working to grow its market share in the data centre chip business long dominated by Nvidia. Alphabet's investment suggests confidence that Qualcomm can carve out a meaningful position as demand for AI computing hardware keeps growing.

Sources: BigGo Finance

Key concepts in this story
Equity stake valuation gain/loss
A reminder: when a company buys shares in another firm, any rise or fall in that firm's share price creates a paper gain or loss on the investor's books, even if no shares are actually sold.
Custom chip
A reminder: this is a chip designed for a specific purpose, like Qualcomm's data centre processors, rather than a general-purpose chip, often to get better performance or efficiency for one type of task.
Market share
A reminder: this is the percentage of total sales in an industry that one company controls. If data centre chip sales total $100 billion and Qualcomm sells $5 billion worth, its market share is 5%.
Why it matters for you

Spotting which companies are quietly building stakes in rivals or suppliers is a useful skill for equity research roles, since it often signals where an industry is heading before it becomes obvious. It also ties into today's chip supply stories: more competition for Nvidia could reshape who controls AI's hardware stack.

SpaceX Reportedly Nearing $60 Billion Deal to Acquire AI Coding Startup Cursor

Elon Musk's rocket company is said to be closing in on a takeover of the popular AI programming tool, a huge leap outside its core business.

SpaceX is reportedly nearing an acquisition of Cursor, the AI-powered coding assistant startup, in a deal valued at around $60 billion, according to Stocktwits. The AI startup is said to be outlining rebranding plans as part of the discussions.

An acquisition is when one company buys another outright, taking full control rather than just investing in it. Cursor is considered a vertical AI company, meaning it builds AI tools tailored to a specific task, in this case software coding, rather than a general-purpose assistant.

If confirmed, the deal would be a striking move for SpaceX, better known for rockets and satellites, into software and AI tooling. It would also mark one of the largest acquisitions of an AI startup to date, underscoring how highly investors are still valuing specialised AI products despite broader market jitters.

Sources: Stocktwits

Key concepts in this story
Acquisition
A reminder: this is when one company buys another outright and takes full control of it, as opposed to simply investing in a portion of its shares.
Vertical AI
A reminder: this describes AI products built for one specific industry or task, like coding or legal work, rather than general-purpose tools that can handle almost any request.
Valuation
A reminder: this is an estimate of what a company is worth, here reportedly around $60 billion for Cursor, usually based on recent funding rounds or expected future earnings.
Why it matters for you

This story is a good example of how AI tools are becoming valuable enough to attract buyers from completely unrelated industries. For anyone in software or AI product roles, it's a reminder that specialised, 'vertical' tools can command enormous valuations if they solve a specific problem extremely well.

Code corner

Tracking Anthropic's Wobbly IPO Valuation Estimates

Based on: Anthropic's IPO Valuation Talk Slides as Low as $1.1 Trillion Amid AI Stock Rout

Different outlets have reported wildly different numbers for Anthropic's possible IPO valuation. Let's use a simple Python dictionary to compare them and work out the size of the swing.

valuations = {
    "Forbes": 1.3e12,
    "KuCoin": 1.1e12,
    "TradingView": 1.5e12,
}

highest = max(valuations.values())
lowest = min(valuations.values())
drop_pct = (highest - lowest) / highest * 100

for source, value in valuations.items():
    print(f"{source}: ${value/1e12:.1f} trillion")

print(f"\nRange: ${lowest/1e12:.1f}T to ${highest/1e12:.1f}T")
print(f"Biggest reported drop: {drop_pct:.1f}%")
Line by line
  1. `valuations = {...}` creates a dictionary mapping each news source's name to its reported valuation estimate in dollars, written using scientific notation (1.3e12 means 1.3 trillion).
  2. `max(valuations.values())` and `min(valuations.values())` pull out just the numeric values from the dictionary and find the highest and lowest figures reported.
  3. `drop_pct = (highest - lowest) / highest * 100` calculates the percentage difference between the highest and lowest estimates, showing how big the swing between reports really is.
  4. The `for` loop goes through each key-value pair in the dictionary and prints it, dividing by `1e12` and formatting to one decimal place so the trillions are readable.
  5. The final two `print` statements summarise the overall range and the size of the biggest reported drop as a percentage.

Try it: Add a fourth source, such as "OpenTools", with its own valuation estimate, then rerun the code. See how adding a new data point changes the calculated range and the percentage drop.
