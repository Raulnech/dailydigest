Nvidia board launches probe into Jensen Huang's ties to alleged Chinese spy

The chipmaker's CEO has admitted to an affair with a former executive reportedly recruited by China's intelligence service.

Nvidia's board of directors announced on Sunday that it is opening an independent investigation after reports surfaced that CEO Jensen Huang was the target of a Chinese espionage operation. In a memo released by the company, Huang admitted to an affair with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based chip company Megaspeed, saying he had been unfaithful to his wife and had "fallen short" of his responsibilities to Nvidia.

A memo circulating among US policymakers earlier this year alleged that Alice Huang had been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security specifically to get close to Nvidia's chief executive. The timing is sensitive: Nvidia's chips sit at the centre of the US-China AI rivalry, and Washington has repeatedly tightened export controls on the kind of advanced chips Nvidia makes, worried they could help China catch up in artificial intelligence and military technology.

Nvidia's board said it has "no reason to believe" that national security or trade secrets were compromised, and that Huang "fully supports" the review. Independent directors, working with outside legal counsel, will carry out the investigation. The episode is a reminder that in an industry this strategically important, a chief executive's personal life can quickly become a matter of national security.

Sources: TechCrunch · The Verge · Tech

Key concepts in this story
Board of directors
A group of people elected by shareholders to oversee a company's management, including hiring, firing and investigating the chief executive if needed.
Independent investigation
A review carried out by people with no personal stake in the outcome, here independent board members and outside lawyers, so the findings are seen as credible rather than a cover-up.
Export controls (reminder)
Government rules restricting which technologies, like advanced AI chips, can be sold to certain countries, used to slow rivals' military or AI progress.
Why it matters for you

This story shows how corporate governance, geopolitics and AI competition increasingly overlap, creating demand for compliance, legal and risk roles inside tech firms. In an interview, you could note how it illustrates 'key person risk' at companies whose products are also considered dual-use national security assets.

The AI industry

Google expands in-house AI chip lineup as Nvidia faces distraction

New specialised chip variants aim to chip away at Nvidia's dominance just as its CEO deals with a spying scandal.

Google has reportedly launched new specialised versions of its in-house AI chips, broadening a lineup it has used for years to train and run its own AI models more cheaply than renting Nvidia hardware. The expansion comes as Nvidia, the dominant supplier of AI chips, is distracted by the investigation into CEO Jensen Huang.

Google's chips, known as TPUs, are custom-built silicon designed specifically for AI workloads rather than general computing, which can make them cheaper to run at scale for the tasks Google needs. Offering more tailored variants could appeal to cloud customers who want chips matched to specific jobs, such as running a trained model to answer queries rather than training it from scratch.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · Yahoo Finance · Global Banking & Finance Review

Key concepts in this story
Custom chip (TPU) (reminder)
A Tensor Processing Unit is a chip Google designed itself specifically for AI tasks, rather than a general-purpose processor, similar to how a specialist tool does one job better than a Swiss army knife.
Inference (reminder)
The stage where a trained AI model is actually used to answer questions or make predictions, as opposed to the earlier 'training' stage where it learns from data.
Why it matters for you

Chip competition between Google and Nvidia affects which cloud platforms are cheapest for AI work, a detail worth knowing if you ever choose infrastructure for a project or discuss cost trade-offs in a technical interview.

The AI industry

Nvidia takes stakes in four Korean AI startups and builds sovereign AI racks with SK Telecom

Nvidia deepens its presence in South Korea with investments and a chip partnership built around its NVLink Fusion technology.

Nvidia has agreed to take equity stakes in four South Korean startups building large language models and robotics software, while also partnering with SK Telecom and local chipmaker Rebellions to build computing systems for Korea's national AI infrastructure. The partnership will use Nvidia's NVLink Fusion technology, which lets different types of chips work together inside the same server rack.

The push fits a wider trend of countries building 'sovereign AI' capacity, meaning computing power and models they control domestically rather than renting from foreign cloud providers. For Nvidia, taking equity stakes alongside selling chips deepens its role from supplier to investor in national AI strategies, giving it a financial upside if these startups succeed.

Sources: Dealroom · The Korea Times · Engineering.com

Key concepts in this story
Sovereign AI
The idea that a country should build and control its own AI infrastructure and models, rather than depending entirely on foreign companies, similar to how nations want their own power grid rather than relying on a neighbour's.
Equity stake (reminder)
Owning a slice of a company's shares, meaning you gain or lose money as that company's value rises or falls, not just getting paid for selling it products.
Why it matters for you

Nvidia investing directly in customers shows how chip supply and venture investing are merging, a trend worth mentioning if asked about Nvidia's business model beyond selling hardware.

AI & finance

Visa finds AI shopping agents fail 43% of test runs

A new benchmark shows AI models still struggle to complete realistic online shopping tasks on their own.

Visa has put a range of AI models through realistic shopping scenarios to test 'agentic commerce,' where an AI agent buys things on a person's behalf. The models failed 43% of the test runs, according to the benchmark.

The result is a reminder that letting AI handle real purchases and real money is still unreliable, even as banks and retailers race to build these features. For now, the data suggests agentic shopping needs much tighter guardrails before it can be trusted with a customer's card.

Sources: Finextra Research Headlines

Key concepts in this story
AI agent (reminder)
A piece of software that uses an AI model to take multi-step actions on its own, like searching for a product, comparing prices and completing checkout, rather than just answering a question.
Eval
A structured test used to measure how well an AI model performs a task, similar to a driving test checking whether a learner can actually drive safely, not just recite the rules.
Why it matters for you

This matters for anyone building or evaluating AI products in fintech, since it shows why companies run evals before trusting an AI agent with money, a concept likely to come up if you work in product or risk teams.

RBA says AI will reshape jobs but stay roughly inflation neutral

Australia's central bank expects short-term price pressure from the AI boom even as it predicts a broadly neutral long-run effect.

The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) said it expects artificial intelligence to significantly affect employment but to be broadly neutral for inflation over time. However, an RBA official reportedly said the AI boom could add near-term pressure on consumer prices (CPI), partly through higher demand for energy and construction tied to data centres.

Sources: AFR · Bloomberg.com

Key concepts in this story
Central bank
A country's institution responsible for managing interest rates and monetary policy, like the RBA in Australia or the Bank of England in the UK, often watching how new technologies affect jobs and prices.
Technological unemployment (reminder)
Job losses caused by machines or software automating tasks humans used to do, a concern central banks track when assessing AI's economic impact.
Why it matters for you

Knowing that central banks are actively modelling AI's effect on jobs and inflation is useful context for finance interviews, since it links directly to interest rate decisions that affect markets.

AI & finance

US margin debt hits record $1.502 trillion

Investors are borrowing more than ever to buy stocks, new FINRA data show.

Margin debt on Wall Street, the amount investors have borrowed against their portfolios to buy more securities, hit a record $1.502 trillion, according to FINRA data. The surge comes as AI-related stocks have driven much of this year's market gains.

Sources: JournalArta

Key concepts in this story
Margin debt
Money an investor borrows from a broker, using their existing shares as collateral, to buy even more stock; if prices fall, they may be forced to sell quickly to repay the loan.
Leverage
Using borrowed money to increase the size of an investment, which magnifies both gains and losses, like putting down a small deposit to buy a much bigger house.
Why it matters for you

Record margin debt is a classic warning sign watched by risk analysts, since heavily leveraged markets can fall faster in a downturn; it connects directly to ongoing worries about an AI-driven market correction.

Google takes over Crusoe's unfinished $30.9bn Wyoming data centre

The tech giant has reportedly acquired a stalled AI infrastructure project from startup Crusoe at an undisclosed price.

Google has reportedly acquired an unfinished data centre project in southeastern Wyoming from AI infrastructure startup Crusoe, a facility earlier valued at around $30.9bn compared with an initial $17bn estimate. The purchase price was not disclosed.

The move shows hyperscale cloud companies like Google increasingly buying distressed or stalled infrastructure projects outright, rather than just signing contracts to rent capacity, as they race to secure the data centre space needed to run AI models.

Sources: The Hans India · shattered.io · NewsBytes

Key concepts in this story
Acquisition (reminder)
When one company buys another company or its assets outright, as opposed to simply partnering with or investing a minority stake in it.
Data centre (reminder)
A large building full of computer servers that stores data and runs the calculations behind AI models and websites, requiring huge amounts of electricity and cooling.
Why it matters for you

It signals how fierce the competition for AI computing capacity has become, with big tech firms willing to buy unfinished projects rather than wait, relevant if you're tracking data centre or infrastructure careers.

The AI startup reportedly joined the ranks of 'unicorn' companies in a new funding round.

AI startup Traversal has raised $120 million in a funding round that reportedly values the company at $1 billion. Details on investors and the startup's specific product were not included in available reporting.

Valuation (reminder)

Code corner

Simulating an AI shopping agent eval

Based on: Visa finds AI shopping agents fail 43% of test runs

Visa's benchmark found AI shopping agents fail 43% of test runs. Here's a tiny simulation of how such an eval works: we send a fake 'LLM response' for a shopping task and check if it meets the rules, just like a simplified version of Visa's test.

import random

def fake_llm_response(task):
    # Pretend this came back from an LLM API call
    outcomes = [
        {"action": "buy", "item": "blue trainers", "price": 45.00},
        {"action": "buy", "item": "blue trainers", "price": 145.00},
        {"action": "buy", "item": "red trainers", "price": 45.00},
    ]
    return random.choice(outcomes)

def check_result(task, result):
    correct_item = task["item"]
    max_price = task["max_price"]
    if result["item"] != correct_item:
        return False, "wrong item"
    if result["price"] > max_price:
        return False, "over budget"
    return True, "success"

task = {"item": "blue trainers", "max_price": 60.00}

runs = 20
failures = 0
for i in range(runs):
    result = fake_llm_response(task)
    passed, reason = check_result(task, result)
    if not passed:
        failures += 1

fail_rate = failures / runs * 100
print(f"Failed {failures} of {runs} runs ({fail_rate:.0f}% failure rate)")
Line by line
  1. `fake_llm_response` stands in for a real API call to an LLM; it randomly returns one of three possible shopping outcomes, so you can run this without needing an API key.
  2. `check_result` is the eval logic: it checks whether the agent bought the right item and stayed within budget, returning True/False plus a reason.
  3. The loop runs the fake agent 20 times and counts how many runs fail, mimicking how Visa tested models across many scenarios rather than just once.
  4. The final print statement calculates a failure rate, the same kind of number (43%) reported in today's Visa story.

Try it: Change `outcomes` in fake_llm_response so only one option ever passes the check, then rerun and see how the failure rate changes. Try adding a fourth possible outcome where the agent buys two pairs instead of one, and update check_result to catch that as a new kind of failure.
