The AI industry

Nvidia ploughs another $3bn into Meta as AI agent race heats up

The chipmaker is buying into Meta's equity raise while the two firms expand work on Meta's new AI agent, Muse.

Nvidia said on Tuesday it will invest $3 billion in Meta as part of the social media giant's newly announced $95 billion fundraising drive for AI infrastructure. The investment deepens an already close relationship between the world's most valuable chipmaker and one of its biggest customers.

Alongside the cash, the two companies are expanding their technical partnership. Meta will roll out more of Nvidia's Vera Rubin and Vera CPU hardware, and the firms will deepen collaboration on Muse, Meta's AI agent that launched last month. An AI agent is software that can understand a goal, plan steps, use external tools and carry out tasks largely without a human guiding every move — Nvidia's chief executive Jensen Huang called it 'a new computing paradigm'.

This is part of a pattern: Nvidia has taken part in 121 investment rounds this year alone, up from just 13 in 2022, putting money into OpenAI, Anthropic, Intel and xAI among others. Critics have flagged these deals as a form of circular financing, where Nvidia's cash helps customers buy more Nvidia chips, inflating demand figures on both sides of the deal. Shares in both companies were flat in early trading despite the announcement.

Sources: Tech · Reuters · grafa.com

Key concepts in this story
AI agent
Reminder: software that can plan and carry out multi-step tasks on a user's behalf, rather than just answering a single question.
Circular financing
Reminder: when a chip supplier invests in a customer who then uses that money to buy the supplier's own chips, making demand look stronger than it organically is.
Vendor financing
A deal where a supplier effectively helps fund its own customer's purchases, for example by taking an equity stake or offering credit, so the customer keeps buying the supplier's products.
Why it matters for you

Understanding these cross-investments helps you spot whether AI revenue growth is genuine demand or partly recycled money between a handful of firms — a sharp interview talking point. It also signals strong hiring demand for roles supporting AI agent infrastructure and partnership deal teams.

Microsoft offers buyouts to 60,000 staff as AI spending surges

Up to a quarter of Microsoft's global workforce has been offered voluntary exit packages as the company balances AI investment with cost control.

Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to as many as 60,000 employees across the US, UK, Canada and India, according to an internal memo from chief people officer Amy Coleman. The offer spans teams including AI experiences, Copilot, CoreAI, Windows, Xbox, finance and HR, though some divisions such as commercial sales, security and quantum are excluded entirely.

The move follows a 4,800-person layoff round earlier this year and a separate, smaller voluntary retirement offer limited to about 9,000 long-serving staff. Microsoft insists this new programme is not based on age, tenure or performance, and is being framed internally as a way to help the company 'focus on the work that matters most' while it pours tens of billions into AI infrastructure.

The timing matters: Microsoft is one of several hyperscalers — companies that run enormous cloud computing platforms — racing to build AI data centres while trying to keep costs under control. Trimming headcount through voluntary exits, rather than forced layoffs, lets the company reduce operating expenses without the reputational cost of mass redundancies, even as its capital spending on chips and data centres keeps climbing.

Sources: The Verge · Tech · Reuters

Key concepts in this story
Automation
Reminder: using software or machines to do tasks that previously needed a person, which can reduce the number of workers a company needs for certain roles.
Technological unemployment
Reminder: job losses that happen because new technology, such as AI, can do work more cheaply or quickly than people.
Operating expenses (opex)
The day-to-day costs of running a business, like salaries and office costs, as opposed to capital expenditure (capex), which is spending on long-term assets like data centres.
Why it matters for you

This is a live example of how AI investment and workforce strategy collide — a good case study for interview questions about how companies fund AI build-outs. If you're job hunting, it's worth knowing which teams are protected (like security) versus exposed (like generalist support roles).

Meta plans $95bn raise to fund AI 'superclusters'

The social media giant will tap equity markets twice as its AI capital spending forecast keeps climbing.

Meta said on Monday it plans to raise $95 billion to fund its AI infrastructure and 'superintelligence' ambitions. The plan has two stages: an initial $35 billion through underwritten public equity offerings, followed by up to $60 billion more through an at-the-market programme, where a company sells new shares gradually into the market over time rather than all at once.

Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom have all said they are interested in buying up to $5 billion of the new shares between them. The raise comes days after Meta reported third-quarter revenue up 26% year-on-year to $64.6 billion, but investors focused instead on negative free cash flow and a higher capital expenditure forecast, now $135–145 billion for the year, sending the stock lower.

Meta is building four 'superclusters', each with more than a gigawatt of computing capacity, as it aims to nearly double its total infrastructure next year. It plans to fund this mix of operating cash flow, investment-grade debt and equity — following Alphabet, SpaceX and Anthropic, which are all also raising huge sums for AI infrastructure. Analysts say the scale of spending reflects an intensifying 'arms race' among AI companies for compute.

Sources: Tech · Financial Times · The Times of India

Key concepts in this story
Capital expenditure (capex)
Reminder: money spent on long-term assets such as data centres and chips, as opposed to day-to-day running costs.
At-the-market offering
A way for a company to sell new shares gradually over time at prevailing market prices, rather than all at once in a single big sale.
Free cash flow
The cash a company has left after paying for operations and investments; negative free cash flow means it's spending more cash than it brings in, often because of heavy capex.
Why it matters for you

Knowing the difference between capex, debt and equity financing is core knowledge for any finance or fintech interview, especially when discussing how 'AI bubble' fears are assessed. It also links directly to today's Anthropic and Broadcom financing stories — all three show how the AI boom is being funded by a mix of debt and equity rather than pure profit.

Banks and Blackstone assemble $63bn to fund Broadcom chips for Anthropic

A consortium including Bank of America, Citi and Morgan Stanley has structured a landmark financing deal to expand AI compute capacity.

Wall Street banks and asset manager Blackstone have lined up $63 billion in financing to help pay for Broadcom AI chips destined for Anthropic, according to Reuters. Bank of America, Citi, Morgan Stanley and Blackstone are named as partners in the deal, described as a 'landmark financing structure' for enabling AI compute capacity.

Deals like this increasingly rely on special purpose vehicles — separate legal entities set up specifically to hold debt and assets for one project — so that the financing doesn't sit directly on a company's own balance sheet. This lets AI labs and hyperscalers access huge sums for chips and data centres without the debt showing up as clearly in their own accounts.

The financing underscores how compute deals, where companies secure large volumes of chips or cloud capacity often years in advance, are increasingly stitched together with complex debt structures rather than straightforward purchases. With few other details disclosed, it remains to be seen how the financing is structured and who ultimately bears the credit risk if demand for AI compute slows.

Sources: Reuters · TradingView

Key concepts in this story
Special purpose vehicle
Reminder: a separate legal entity created to hold specific assets and debts, keeping them off the main company's balance sheet.
GPU-backed loan
Reminder: a loan where the graphics processing units (chips) themselves are used as collateral, similar to how a house secures a mortgage.
Compute deal
Reminder: an agreement to secure a large amount of computing power or chips, often worth billions and arranged years ahead of actual use.
Why it matters for you

These complex financing structures are becoming the norm for AI infrastructure, so understanding special purpose vehicles and off-balance-sheet debt is valuable for anyone eyeing finance or risk roles connected to tech.

AI & finance

Bank of England and FCA set out plans for AI and 'agentic finance'

UK regulators are testing how AI agents could reshape data sharing across financial services.

The Bank of England and the Financial Conduct Authority have published a joint report on the use of AI in UK financial services, according to reports. Part of the focus is on 'agentic finance' — the idea that AI agents could soon handle tasks like comparing products or moving money on a customer's behalf, rather than people doing it manually through apps.

As part of this, the FCA is reportedly planning to test new 'Open Finance' infrastructure models. Open Finance extends the idea of Open Banking — where banks share customer data securely with other approved providers, with the customer's consent — to a wider range of financial products, such as pensions, savings and insurance.

The reports suggest regulators are trying to get ahead of a shift where AI agents interact directly with financial systems, rather than waiting until the technology is already widespread. Advice firms and other financial institutions are reportedly being urged to check whether their own systems and controls are ready for this kind of agentic activity.

Sources: Global Regulation Tomorrow · Open Banking Expo · QA Financial

Key concepts in this story
Regulator
Reminder: a body like the FCA or Bank of England that sets and enforces rules for an industry, in this case UK financial services.
Agentic AI
Reminder: AI systems built to take actions and complete tasks with some independence, rather than simply answering questions.
Open Finance
An extension of Open Banking that lets customers share more of their financial data (like pensions or insurance), with consent, across different providers so they get better comparisons or automated services.
Why it matters for you

Fintech and compliance roles increasingly need people who understand both AI agents and data-sharing rules like Open Banking and Open Finance — a useful area to mention if you're applying to a bank's innovation or regulatory team.

Anthropic's IPO filing reveals hypergrowth and an $80bn-scale loss

The AI lab's 240,000-word filing lays bare the costs of competing at the frontier of AI development.

Anthropic's filing ahead of its initial public offering (IPO) — the process of a private company selling shares to the public for the first time — has reportedly revealed rapid revenue growth alongside very heavy losses, which some reports put at a scale of around $80 billion. The filing, covering roughly 240,000 words, is being described by the Financial Times as 'bold but sane' in how it frames the risks of building frontier AI models.

Frontier AI labs like Anthropic spend enormous sums on compute — the computing power needed to train and run large models — well ahead of the revenue those models eventually generate. Reports suggest the filing details both hypergrowth in revenue and candid warnings about AI safety risks, reflecting the tension between racing to build powerful models and acknowledging their dangers.

The filing gives outsiders their clearest look yet at the economics of a top AI lab: how much it costs to train frontier models, how fast enterprise demand is growing, and how exposed the business is to swings in compute costs. As Anthropic heads towards a public listing, investors will be weighing whether this growth can outpace the losses before public market patience runs out.

Sources: Financial Times · Finextra Research · Forbes

Key concepts in this story
IPO (initial public offering)
Reminder: the first time a private company sells shares to the public, after which anyone can buy and sell its stock on an exchange.
Net loss
Reminder: when a company's total costs exceed its revenue over a period, meaning it spent more than it earned.
Frontier AI lab
Reminder: a company building the most advanced, largest-scale AI models available, such as OpenAI, Anthropic or Google DeepMind.
Why it matters for you

IPO filings are one of the few places outsiders get real numbers on how AI labs spend money, so reading them is a genuinely useful skill if you want to work in AI investing, research or journalism.

Startups & funding

Itaú Unibanco buys AI startup NeoSpace for around $589m

Brazil's largest private bank is betting that AI trained on financial data beats generic models for banking tasks.

Itaú Unibanco, Brazil's biggest private-sector bank, has agreed to acquire AI startup NeoSpace in an all-cash deal reportedly worth between $580 million and $589 million, according to multiple reports. The purchase reflects a wider trend of banks acquiring specialist AI startups rather than relying solely on general-purpose models from firms like OpenAI or Anthropic.

NeoSpace reportedly focuses on AI built specifically for financial data, an approach sometimes called vertical AI — AI designed and trained for one industry's specific needs, rather than a broad range of tasks. For a bank handling credit decisions, fraud detection and customer data, a model tuned to financial language and risk patterns can outperform a general-purpose chatbot.

The deal size is notable for a Latin American AI startup acquisition, and signals how seriously major banks are taking the build-versus-buy decision on AI capability. Full financial and strategic details of the deal have not been disclosed beyond the reported price tag.

Sources: AI Insider · Dealroom · Tech Times

Key concepts in this story
Acquisition
Reminder: when one company buys another, usually taking full ownership and control of its technology, staff and customers.
Vertical AI
Reminder: AI systems built and trained specifically for one industry, such as finance or healthcare, rather than general-purpose use.
Credit data provider
Reminder: a company that supplies data used to assess how likely a borrower is to repay debt, which banks rely on for lending decisions.
Why it matters for you

This shows banks are increasingly buying specialist AI startups rather than building everything in-house — useful context if you're considering a career bridging fintech and AI, where 'vertical AI' skills are in high demand.

Intel to buy Israeli quantum software startup Classiq for about $2.3bn

The chipmaker is expanding beyond classical computing with a deal to build a full-stack quantum computing platform.

Intel has agreed to acquire Classiq, an Israeli quantum computing software startup, in a deal reportedly valued at around $2.2–2.3 billion, according to multiple reports including Bloomberg and CTech. The acquisition is intended to help Intel build what it calls a 'full-stack' quantum computing platform, combining hardware and software under one roof.

Quantum computing uses the unusual behaviour of particles at very small scales to process certain types of calculations far faster than traditional computers, though the technology is still largely experimental. Classiq makes software tools that help developers design and run quantum algorithms, a layer Intel reportedly lacked as it builds out its own quantum hardware ambitions.

Reports suggest the deal follows years of personal interest in quantum computing from Intel chief executive Lip-Bu Tan. The purchase price marks one of the largest exits for an Israeli quantum startup to date, reflecting growing investor appetite for quantum computing even as the AI boom dominates headlines.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · Bloomberg.com · KuCoin

Key concepts in this story
Acquisition
Reminder: when one company buys another, taking over its technology, staff and operations.
Quantum computing
A type of computing that uses quantum physics to process information in ways that can, for certain problems, be far faster than normal computers — still an emerging, mostly experimental technology.
Full AI stack
Reminder: here applied to quantum, meaning owning every layer of a technology — from the physical hardware up to the software that developers use — rather than just one piece of it.
Why it matters for you

Quantum computing is a smaller but growing parallel story to the AI boom, and knowing the basics helps you follow conversations about 'what comes after AI' in tech investing circles.

Code corner

Breaking down a multi-billion-dollar capital raise

Based on: Meta plans $95bn raise to fund AI 'superclusters'

Meta's $95bn AI raise is split between an upfront equity offering and a slower at-the-market programme. Here's a tiny Python script that models a capital raise as a mix of funding sources and prints out the percentage breakdown — the same kind of calculation an analyst might do on a bank's deal team.

raise_plan = {
    "public_equity_offering": 35_000_000_000,
    "at_the_market_program": 60_000_000_000,
}

total_raise = sum(raise_plan.values())

print(f"Total planned raise: ${total_raise:,}")
print()

for source, amount in raise_plan.items():
    share = amount / total_raise * 100
    print(f"{source:25s} ${amount:>15,}  ({share:5.1f}%)")

# Quick sanity check: does Nvidia/AMD/Broadcom interest
# (up to $5bn) fit inside the first tranche?
chip_firms_interest = 5_000_000_000
fits = chip_firms_interest <= raise_plan["public_equity_offering"]
print()
print(f"Chip firms' $5bn interest fits in first tranche: {fits}")
Line by line
  1. `raise_plan = {...}` creates a dictionary mapping each funding source to its dollar amount, using underscores in numbers purely so humans can read them (35_000_000_000 is the same as 35000000000).
  2. `total_raise = sum(raise_plan.values())` adds up all the dictionary's values to get the grand total, $95 billion.
  3. The `for source, amount in raise_plan.items():` loop goes through each key-value pair so we can calculate what percentage of the whole raise each source represents.
  4. `f"{source:25s} ${amount:>15,} ({share:5.1f}%)"` is an f-string with formatting codes: `:25s` pads the text to 25 characters, `:>15,` right-aligns the number with comma separators, and `:5.1f` shows one decimal place — this keeps the printed table neatly aligned.
  5. The final section checks whether the chip firms' reported $5bn interest could be covered just by the first $35bn tranche, returning a simple True/False answer.

Try it: Add a third funding source called 'debt_financing' worth $20 billion, update total_raise automatically, and see how the percentages shift once there are three sources instead of two.
