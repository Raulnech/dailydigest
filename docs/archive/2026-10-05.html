The AI industry

Nadella bets Microsoft's future on selling AI 'by the token'

Eleven years after rescuing Microsoft with the cloud, Satya Nadella is trying to repeat the trick by turning Copilot into the backbone of a token-based AI business.

At a closed-door event in Seattle last month, Microsoft chief executive Satya Nadella told roughly 50 corporate clients that the AI shift inside companies has to happen from every direction at once — 'bottom up, top down, and middle up'. It was a glimpse of how the 59-year-old executive, who has spent over half his life at Microsoft, is trying to steer the company through its second great platform shift in a decade.

Nadella made his name after taking over from Steve Ballmer in 2014, ditching one-off software licences for subscription cloud services and turning Azure into a cash machine. Now he wants Microsoft clients to pay based on how much computing they actually use, measured in tokens — the chunks of text an AI model reads and generates. GitHub Copilot, the coding assistant built with OpenAI, is the test case: after switching to consumption-based pricing earlier this year, despite early customer grumbling, its user base nearly doubled to 50 million and sales growth reportedly accelerated 60% quarter-on-quarter.

The stakes are high because Microsoft, despite a near-$4 trillion market capitalisation, has not established itself as the industry's clear AI leader. Nvidia dominates chips, OpenAI and Anthropic lead on frontier models, and Microsoft's own alliance with OpenAI has reportedly cooled. Investors remain unconvinced: the stock is up just 7% this year, trailing its megacap rivals.

Nadella's pitch to Wall Street is that AI will expand Microsoft's 'total addressable market' — industry jargon for the total pool of revenue a company could capture — by turning every employee into a buyer of computing power rather than just software seats. Whether that bet pays off, as with the cloud shift a decade ago, will become clearer as Microsoft pushes Copilot deeper into coding, security and everyday office work.

Sources: Tech · The Tech Buzz · fool.com

Key concepts in this story
Token (in AI)
A token is a small chunk of text — often a word or part of one — that an AI model processes. Companies increasingly charge for AI use based on how many tokens go in and out, similar to paying for data by the megabyte.
Consumption-based pricing
Instead of a flat subscription fee, customers pay for exactly how much of a service they use. It's like an electricity bill rather than a fixed monthly gym membership.
Total addressable market (TAM)
TAM is the total revenue a company could theoretically earn if it captured 100% of a market. If every office worker in the world eventually buys AI tools, the TAM for those tools is enormous.
Agentic coding
This refers to AI tools that don't just suggest code but can carry out multi-step programming tasks on their own, such as writing, testing and fixing a feature with minimal human input.
Why it matters for you

This story matters for anyone eyeing product, sales or engineering roles at big tech firms, since consumption-based AI pricing is becoming the industry norm and interviewers will expect you to explain how it differs from subscriptions. It also links to today's robotics and chip-infrastructure stories, since all of them hinge on turning raw computing power into billable AI products.

The AI industry

Samsung puts $1 billion into AI infrastructure venture backed by KKR and Nvidia

The South Korean electronics giant is joining investment firm KKR and chipmaker Nvidia in backing a company building out AI computing capacity.

Samsung has reportedly committed $1 billion to an AI infrastructure firm that also counts private equity giant KKR and chip designer Nvidia among its backers, according to the Wall Street Journal. Full financial details of the deal have not been disclosed.

The investment fits a broader pattern of established tech and finance players funnelling money into firms that build and operate the data centres and chip capacity needed to run AI models, rather than building AI software themselves.

With demand for AI computing continuing to outstrip supply, such infrastructure bets are becoming one of the most common ways large companies are positioning themselves in the AI boom.

Sources: WSJ

Key concepts in this story
AI infrastructure
This covers the physical building blocks of AI — chips, data centres and the power and cooling systems that keep them running — as opposed to the AI software or models themselves.
Strategic investor
A strategic investor puts money into a company not purely for financial return but because the deal supports its own business, such as Samsung securing future access to AI computing capacity.
Why it matters for you

It shows that hardware giants, not just software firms, are racing to secure a stake in AI infrastructure — useful context if you're asked in an interview who actually profits from the AI boom beyond the well-known model makers.

The AI industry

Chinese AI labs reportedly close the gap on OpenAI and Anthropic

New reporting suggests Chinese developers are narrowing the performance lead held by top US AI labs.

Chinese AI laboratories are reportedly closing the gap with US leaders OpenAI and Anthropic, according to reports from TradingView and Bloomberg, though specific benchmark results were not detailed in the available coverage.

The claim fits a wider trend this year of Chinese labs releasing increasingly capable models, often as open-weight releases that let developers download and run them freely, challenging the idea that only a handful of well-funded US labs can build frontier-level AI.

If the trend continues, it could reshape competitive dynamics in the AI industry, intensifying pressure on US labs and complicating the debate over export controls on advanced chips to China.

Sources: TradingView · Bloomberg.com

Key concepts in this story
Frontier AI lab
A frontier lab is one of the small number of organisations — such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, or now some Chinese labs — pushing the absolute cutting edge of model capability.
Open-weight model
An open-weight model has its internal parameters published so anyone can download and run it themselves, unlike closed models accessed only through an API.
Benchmark
A benchmark is a standard test used to compare AI models, like a set exam that lets you rank different students on the same scale.
Why it matters for you

Competitive pressure from Chinese labs could accelerate price cuts and open-weight releases, which is good news if you want to experiment with AI cheaply, but it also raises the geopolitical stakes around chip export controls covered in recent digests.

AI & finance

South Korean banks hit by wave of AI-assisted cyberattacks

President Seoul orders a full investigation after hackers reportedly used AI tools to breach major banks and leak customer data.

South Korea's major banks came under sustained cyberattack reportedly aided by artificial intelligence, prompting the country's president to order a thorough investigation into the breaches, according to multiple outlets including the Straits Times and Korea Times. Financial institutions and brokerages have since tightened security measures.

Local reports describe the incidents as exposing how far behind Korea's financial cyber defences have fallen compared with the sophistication of AI-enabled attackers. Personal data management at financial firms has come under particular scrutiny, with regulators reportedly probing how the leaks occurred.

The episode adds to a string of incidents globally in which AI tools have lowered the skill threshold needed to mount convincing attacks, from automated phishing to faster scanning for weaknesses in bank systems.

With the government demanding answers, expect tighter cybersecurity rules for South Korean financial firms and renewed scrutiny of how banks worldwide are using — or failing to defend against — AI in their security operations.

Sources: 매일경제 · The Straits Times · The420.in

Key concepts in this story
AI-driven hacking
This is the use of AI tools to automate or improve cyberattacks, such as writing convincing phishing emails or scanning systems for weaknesses far faster than a human could.
Data breach
A data breach happens when sensitive information, like customer account details, is accessed or leaked without authorisation — similar to a burglar getting into a filing cabinet full of personal records.
Zero-day vulnerability
A zero-day is a software flaw that's unknown to the people who could fix it, giving attackers a window to exploit it before any patch exists.
Why it matters for you

Banks everywhere are hiring for AI-security and fraud-detection roles, so understanding how AI both threatens and defends financial systems is a strong talking point in fintech interviews.

Japan's 30-year bond yield hits record 4.235% as AI debt mounts

Long-term borrowing costs in Japan reached a new high, as concerns grow over the debt being used to finance the AI buildout.

Japan's 30-year government bond yield climbed to a record 4.235%, according to a report from Startup Fortune, as investors grow wary of the rising pile of debt being used to fund AI infrastructure projects worldwide.

Separately, Moneycontrol reported that leveraged trading in Indian stocks has fuelled a $33 billion debt binge, another sign that borrowed money is flowing heavily into markets riding the AI boom.

Rising bond yields matter because they push up borrowing costs across the economy, from mortgages to corporate loans, and can make highly leveraged bets — including AI infrastructure financed with debt — more expensive and riskier to sustain.

Sources: Startup Fortune · Moneycontrol.com

Key concepts in this story
Bond yield
A bond yield is the annual return an investor gets for lending money to a government or company. When yields rise, it usually means investors want more compensation for the risk of holding that debt.
Leverage (margin debt)
Leverage means using borrowed money to make a bigger bet than your own cash would allow — for example, borrowing £9 to add to £1 of your own money to buy £10 of stock, which multiplies both gains and losses.
AI bubble
This is the worry that enthusiasm for AI has pushed valuations and spending far beyond what current revenues can justify, echoing past tech manias that ended in sharp corrections.
Why it matters for you

Rising global bond yields and debt-fuelled AI spending are exactly the kind of macro signals analysts at banks are expected to connect to tech valuations, making this a good story to reference when discussing systemic risk in interviews.

SpaceX prices record-breaking IPO at $135 a share, valuing it at $1.75 trillion

Elon Musk's rocket company has gone public in the largest stock market debut ever, immediately buying AI coding startup Cursor for $60 billion.

SpaceX priced its initial public offering (IPO) at $135 a share, reportedly making it the largest IPO in history and valuing the company at $1.75 trillion, according to TradingKey. The stock reportedly jumped 19% on its first day of trading.

Days after listing, SpaceX reportedly moved to acquire AI coding startup Cursor for $60 billion, a sign that the newly public company intends to use its enormous valuation and cash position to expand aggressively into AI software, not just rockets and satellites.

The scale of the listing has rippled across markets: reports suggest investors sold shares in Tesla, another Musk-led company, to free up cash to chase a potential first-day pop in SpaceX stock. Attention now turns to how the shares perform as an August float — an unlock date when more investors are allowed to sell their shares — approaches, which could add selling pressure.

Sources: TradingKey · TradingKey · TradingKey

Key concepts in this story
IPO (initial public offering)
An IPO is the first time a private company sells shares to the public on a stock exchange, turning it into a publicly traded company that anyone can invest in.
S-1 filing
An S-1 is the detailed document a US company must file with regulators before an IPO, disclosing its finances, risks and business model to prospective investors.
Market capitalisation
Market cap is a company's total value on the stock market, calculated by multiplying its share price by the number of shares outstanding. At $135 a share, SpaceX's huge share count pushed its market cap to $1.75 trillion.
Why it matters for you

This is one of the biggest finance stories of the year and a useful case study for understanding how IPO pricing, valuation multiples and acquisitions interact — all core concepts for anyone pursuing equity research or venture roles.

Schneider Electric to buy software firm PTC for $22.6 billion

The French energy management giant is paying a hefty premium to add PTC's industrial software to its portfolio.

Schneider Electric has agreed to acquire US software maker PTC for more than $20 billion, reported at $22.6 billion by Techzine Global and Bloomberg. PTC makes software used by manufacturers to design products and manage industrial equipment.

The deal reflects a broader push by industrial and energy companies to bulk up on software capabilities, as factories and infrastructure increasingly rely on digital tools, data and AI to run efficiently.

While specific terms beyond the price tag were not detailed in available reports, a deal of this size signals strong confidence that industrial software, increasingly infused with AI features, will keep growing in value even as broader tech valuations face scrutiny.

Sources: Bloomberg.com · Techzine Global · trendingtopics.eu

Key concepts in this story
Acquisition
An acquisition is when one company buys another, usually to gain its technology, customers or market position rather than building those things from scratch.
Valuation multiple
This expresses how much a buyer pays relative to a company's revenue or profit — for instance, paying $22.6 billion for a firm with $2 billion in revenue would be roughly an 11x revenue multiple.
Industrial software
This is software used to design, build and manage physical products and factory equipment, as opposed to consumer apps — think of the digital blueprints and control systems behind a car factory.
Why it matters for you

Big industrial acquisitions like this show where traditional engineering firms see AI and software value accumulating, a useful data point if you're weighing a career in industrial tech versus consumer AI.

German robotics startup RobCo hits $1 billion valuation

Investor appetite for 'physical AI' — robots that act in the real world — pushed the modular robotics firm into unicorn territory.

RobCo, a German robotics startup, has reportedly reached a $1 billion valuation, according to the Wall Street Journal, Bloomberg and other outlets, becoming the latest company to benefit from surging investor interest in what's being called 'physical AI' — AI systems that control robots and machinery rather than just generating text or images.

Details of the funding round that produced the new valuation were not fully disclosed in available reports, but the jump reflects a broader trend of investors chasing companies that apply AI techniques to warehouses, factories and other physical environments.

RobCo's rise mirrors a pattern seen across the AI industry this year, where strong demand for automation and robotics has pushed several young companies past the symbolic $1 billion 'unicorn' threshold in quick succession.

Sources: WSJ · Bloomberg.com · konsulteer.com

Key concepts in this story
Unicorn
A unicorn is a privately held startup valued at $1 billion or more — a rare enough feat in traditional venture capital to earn the mythical nickname.
Physical AI
Physical AI refers to artificial intelligence systems that sense and act in the real world, such as warehouse robots or robotic arms, rather than purely digital tasks like writing text.
Funding round
A funding round is a point at which a startup raises new money from investors in exchange for equity, with each round typically setting a new valuation for the company.
Why it matters for you

Robotics and physical AI are emerging as a major new frontier for startup investment, so if you're considering roles outside pure software, this is a sector worth tracking alongside foundation models and fintech.

Code corner

Simulating token-based billing like Copilot

Based on: Nadella bets Microsoft's future on selling AI 'by the token'

Microsoft's Copilot now charges based on how many AI tokens a customer uses, rather than a flat subscription. Here's a tiny simulation of how that billing might work, using a fake AI response instead of a real API call.

import json

# Pretend this is a response we got back from an AI API
fake_api_response = json.dumps({
    "output_text": "Here is a Python function that adds two numbers.",
    "usage": {"input_tokens": 42, "output_tokens": 18}
})

PRICE_PER_1000_INPUT = 0.50   # dollars per 1000 input tokens
PRICE_PER_1000_OUTPUT = 1.50  # dollars per 1000 output tokens

def calculate_bill(response_json):
    data = json.loads(response_json)
    usage = data["usage"]
    input_cost = (usage["input_tokens"] / 1000) * PRICE_PER_1000_INPUT
    output_cost = (usage["output_tokens"] / 1000) * PRICE_PER_1000_OUTPUT
    total = input_cost + output_cost
    return round(total, 6)

cost = calculate_bill(fake_api_response)
print(f"Tokens used: {json.loads(fake_api_response)['usage']}")
print(f"Total cost for this request: ${cost}")
Line by line
  1. `fake_api_response` is a JSON string standing in for what a real AI API would return, including a count of input and output tokens used.
  2. `json.loads` converts that JSON string into a Python dictionary so we can read values out of it, like `data["usage"]`.
  3. The price constants set how much a provider charges per 1000 tokens, mirroring how Copilot and similar tools bill based on consumption rather than a flat fee.
  4. `calculate_bill` pulls out the token counts, applies the per-token prices, and adds input and output costs together to get a total.
  5. The final `print` statements show the token usage and the resulting dollar cost for a single AI request.

Try it: Change the token counts in `fake_api_response` to simulate a much longer conversation (say, 5,000 input tokens and 2,000 output tokens) and see how the cost changes. Then try adding a third price tier for a 'premium' model that costs twice as much per token.
