The AI industry

Big labs, big tech and the direction of travel.

The AI industry

Anthropic and Nscale reportedly plot second $45bn compute deal

Just a month after their first mega-deal, the AI lab and its infrastructure partner are said to be lining up another, even as Nscale's IPO valuation meets investor pushback.

Anthropic and Nscale are reportedly close to agreeing a fresh $45 billion compute deal, according to Bloomberg and Reuters, barely a month after announcing a near-identical arrangement. Sources caution that talks are ongoing and the final terms could still change.

Nscale supplies the vast banks of computing power - often called AI infrastructure - that AI labs need to train and run their models. Anthropic has struck a string of such giant deals over the past year as it races to secure enough capacity to keep pace with rivals like OpenAI. Both companies are also pursuing stock market listings: Anthropic is expected to go public this year, while Nscale is reportedly facing investor resistance over its proposed $30 billion valuation.

The scale of the spending has unnerved some investors, who worry that AI firms are committing enormous sums for computing power without yet proving it generates matching profit. Anthropic is betting the investment will pay off as demand for its models grows, but with both companies' IPOs looming, the deal's terms could shape how public markets judge the wider AI buildout.

Sources: TechCrunch · The Korea Times · Reuters

Key concepts in this story
Compute deal
A long-term contract in which an AI lab pays an infrastructure provider to guarantee access to computing power, similar to a factory signing a multi-year contract with an electricity supplier to ensure it never runs out of power.
Neocloud (reminder)
A cloud provider, like Nscale, that specialises in renting out GPU computing capacity to AI companies rather than offering general-purpose cloud services.
Circular financing
When money flows in a loop between a small group of closely linked companies - for example a chipmaker investing in a cloud provider that then buys chips from it - making it harder to judge whether real external demand exists.
Why it matters for you

Deals like this show how much of the AI industry's apparent growth is actually inter-company spending commitments rather than outside revenue - a distinction worth raising in any finance or strategy interview. It also connects directly to today's CoreWeave story: the same compute-deal model underpins both firms' balance sheets.

The AI industry

CoreWeave's bonds hit 'distressed' territory, dragging down other AI stocks

Investors are demanding much higher yields to hold CoreWeave's debt, reviving fears that the AI buildout is propped up by risky borrowing.

CoreWeave shares fell more than 20% last week after its bonds slipped into so-called distressed territory - meaning investors now demand over 1,000 basis points more yield than they would from safe government bonds. The slide followed investor Chamath Palihapitiya questioning whether the AI buildout is a private credit bubble, and comments from CoreWeave cofounder Brannin McBee about wanting an investment-grade credit rating so the company could borrow from insurers.

CoreWeave pioneered using GPUs as collateral for loans and currently owes more than $35 billion, having lost over $1 billion in the first half of the year on a business that essentially just sells computing capacity. Its defenders point to $103.7 billion in remaining performance obligations - contracted future revenue, about 40% of it due in 2028, mostly from Microsoft, OpenAI and Nvidia - that cannot easily be cancelled.

The anxiety has spread: Oracle's shares and bonds have also fallen amid similar worries about its AI-related debt, and analysts warn the concerns apply to neoclouds generally. Whether this is early evidence of an AI bubble bursting or just a stock-price wobble depends on whether CoreWeave can keep servicing its debt long enough for the AI boom to validate its business model.

Sources: The Verge

Key concepts in this story
Distressed debt / basis points spread
A bond is considered distressed when its yield is at least 1,000 basis points (10 percentage points) above a safe government bond - for example, if government debt yields 4.5% and a company's bonds yield 15%, that's a 1,050 basis-point spread, signalling investors think default is a real risk.
GPU-backed loan
A loan where graphics chips (GPUs) are pledged as collateral, much like a car loan where the car itself secures the debt - if the borrower can't pay, the lender can seize the chips.
Remaining performance obligations (RPO)
The total value of contracted future revenue a company expects to receive, even though it hasn't been paid yet - like a gym having members locked into two-year contracts that guarantee future income.
Junk bond (reminder)
A bond rated below investment-grade, meaning higher risk of default and therefore a higher interest rate to compensate lenders.
Why it matters for you

Understanding bond spreads and remaining performance obligations is core knowledge for credit analyst or risk roles at banks, and a sharp interview answer would note that a falling share price doesn't automatically mean a failing business, just a change in investor confidence.

The AI industry

Amazon launches TorchNeuron to loosen Nvidia's grip on AI software

A new tool lets developers shift AI apps from Nvidia chips to Amazon's Trainium with barely any code changes, challenging Nvidia's software moat.

Amazon Web Services this week released TorchNeuron, a tool that lets developers move AI applications built with PyTorch onto Amazon's own AI chip, Trainium, by changing just one line of configuration. Amazon says about 85% of AI applications are built using PyTorch, a free, open-source toolkit for building and training AI models that is now maintained by the Linux Foundation.

Nvidia's dominance in AI hardware rests heavily on CUDA, its software layer that makes PyTorch run smoothly on Nvidia GPUs, effectively locking many developers into Nvidia hardware by default. Amazon is attacking that advantage on two fronts: offering an easy on-ramp to Trainium, and contributing code directly to the main PyTorch project to strip out code that ties the framework to any one chipmaker.

AWS argues that because Trainium can handle both training (teaching a model) and inference (running a trained model to answer requests), customers save money overall even if its per-request price isn't the cheapest - pointing to voice-AI firm Deepgram processing 70% more audio per dollar after switching. Other chipmakers, including AMD, already offer similar PyTorch portability, but Amazon's push to make PyTorch itself less Nvidia-centric could have far bigger consequences for Nvidia if it succeeds in giving developers genuine hardware choice.

Sources: TechCrunch · Reuters · Yahoo Finance

Key concepts in this story
PyTorch
A free, open-source software toolkit that most AI developers use to build, train and run machine learning models - comparable to a universal set of building blocks that works regardless of which factory (chip) assembles them.
CUDA / vendor lock-in
CUDA is Nvidia's software layer that makes its chips work smoothly with popular AI tools; because so much code is optimised for it, switching away from Nvidia chips becomes difficult, a situation known as vendor lock-in.
Training vs inference (reminder)
Training is the process of teaching a model using large amounts of data; inference is using that trained model afterwards to actually answer questions or complete tasks.
Why it matters for you

Knowing the difference between a chip and the software ecosystem around it is useful for anyone discussing AI infrastructure investment decisions, and this story is a good example to cite when an interviewer asks about risks to Nvidia's dominance.

AI & finance

AI stock selloff wipes out roughly $1 trillion in a week, raising credit-risk questions

A sharp drop across AI-linked shares has put fresh scrutiny on who actually holds the risk in the industry's borrowing binge.

According to a Finextra community piece, AI-related stocks reportedly lost around $1 trillion in combined market value over the course of a week, triggering debate about how much leverage - borrowed money used to amplify investment - now sits underneath the AI industry's rapid growth.

The commentary links the selloff to other warning signs flagged this week, including CoreWeave's distressed bonds and concerns about hyperscalers' hidden debt, suggesting investors are increasingly questioning whether the credit risk behind the AI investment boom is properly understood or disclosed.

Sources: Finextra Research Headlines

Key concepts in this story
Leverage
Using borrowed money to fund investment, which can boost returns when things go well but magnifies losses when they don't - like putting down a 10% deposit on a house and still being on the hook for the full mortgage if its value falls.
Credit risk
The risk that a borrower won't be able to repay its debt, which matters to anyone who lent it money, bought its bonds, or is otherwise financially exposed to it.
Market correction (reminder)
A sharp, short-term fall in asset prices, typically defined as a drop of 10% or more from a recent high.
Why it matters for you

A week like this is exactly the sort of event an interviewer might ask you to explain in plain terms - being able to connect a stock selloff to underlying credit concerns shows you understand markets beyond headlines.

AI & finance

S&P Global warns hyperscalers' real AI debt load may be bigger than disclosed

The ratings agency says tech giants' balance sheets may understate borrowing tied to a roughly $7 trillion AI spending spree.

S&P Global has reportedly warned that hyperscalers - the handful of giant cloud companies, such as Amazon, Microsoft and Google, building most of the world's AI data centres - may be carrying more AI-related debt than is currently visible in their public financial statements.

The concern centres on off-balance-sheet liabilities: financing arrangements such as leases or joint ventures that let a company fund expensive infrastructure like data centres without that debt appearing directly on its own balance sheet. As capital expenditure on AI infrastructure heads toward a reported $7 trillion industry-wide, S&P suggests this could make it harder for investors to judge how exposed these companies truly are if AI spending slows.

Sources: Reuters · The Economic Times · BigGo Finance

Key concepts in this story
Hyperscaler
A very large cloud computing company - like Amazon, Microsoft or Google - that operates vast networks of data centres at a scale few other firms can match.
Off-balance-sheet liabilities
Debts or obligations a company doesn't have to list directly on its balance sheet, often through leasing structures or joint ventures, similar to renting a car with an option to buy rather than taking out a loan to own it outright - the obligation exists, but it looks different on paper.
Capex (reminder)
Capital expenditure, meaning money spent on long-term physical assets like data centres, chips and buildings rather than day-to-day running costs.
Why it matters for you

Spotting hidden liabilities is a core skill for equity and credit analysts, and this story pairs naturally with the CoreWeave piece today - both are about whether disclosed debt tells the full story of AI's financing risks.

AI & finance

AI stocks swell to 49% of the S&P 500, near record concentration

Big Tech's AI bet now accounts for roughly half the value of America's benchmark stock index, echoing historic concentration extremes.

AI-related companies reportedly make up about 49% of the S&P 500's total value, the second-highest level of concentration on record for the index, which tracks 500 of the largest US public companies and is widely used as a benchmark for the overall stock market.

Such heavy concentration means the index's performance now depends heavily on a small cluster of AI-exposed firms. Combined with this week's selloff and CoreWeave's debt troubles, that dependence raises the stakes for pension funds, index trackers and everyday savers whose investments are tied to the S&P 500's fortunes.

Sources: Crypto Briefing

Key concepts in this story
S&P 500 / index fund
The S&P 500 is an index of roughly 500 large US companies; an index fund is an investment that simply buys all the companies in that index in proportion, so its performance mirrors the index as a whole.
Market concentration (reminder)
A situation where a small number of companies account for a disproportionately large share of a market's or index's total value, making overall performance very sensitive to those few firms.
Why it matters for you

If you work in or alongside asset management, understanding concentration risk helps explain why a seemingly diversified index fund can actually be a concentrated bet on AI - a useful point to raise when discussing portfolio risk.

Startups & funding

Chip startup Tenstorrent raises $1.5bn, doubling valuation to $6.2bn

Jim Keller's open-chip venture reportedly turned down acquisition interest from Intel and Qualcomm to raise a Series E instead.

Tenstorrent, the AI chip startup led by veteran chip designer Jim Keller, has raised $1.5 billion in a Series E funding round, more than doubling its valuation to $6.2 billion, according to multiple reports including Reuters and Bloomberg.

The company designs processors using RISC-V, an open and free alternative to the proprietary instruction sets that underpin most existing chips, aiming to build an open compute ecosystem for AI workloads rather than depending on Nvidia or other incumbent chipmakers. Reports suggest Tenstorrent turned down acquisition interest from both Intel and Qualcomm in favour of staying independent and raising this round.

The raise adds Tenstorrent to a growing field of challengers trying to chip away at Nvidia's dominance in AI hardware, alongside efforts like Amazon's Trainium push reported today. Investors appear to be betting that demand for AI computing power is large enough to support multiple competing hardware ecosystems rather than a single winner.

Sources: Data Center Dynamics · Reuters · Bloomberg.com

Key concepts in this story
Series E
A later-stage venture capital funding round, typically for companies that have already raised four earlier rounds (Seed, A, B, C/D) and are scaling an established business rather than just proving an idea works.
RISC-V
A free, open-source set of instructions that computer chips can be built around, unlike proprietary designs owned by companies such as Arm or Intel - comparable to a shared, royalty-free blueprint anyone can use to build a chip.
Valuation (reminder)
An estimate of what a company is worth, often set by what investors are willing to pay for a stake in a funding round.
Why it matters for you

Chip design roles and hardware-software integration skills are in high demand as more players challenge Nvidia; name-checking RISC-V shows interviewers you understand there are real alternatives to proprietary chip ecosystems.

Startups & funding

AI assistant startup Town hits unicorn status just four months after launch

A $108 million Series B values the workplace AI-assistant maker at over $1 billion, underscoring investor appetite for enterprise AI agents.

Town, a San Francisco startup building AI assistants for businesses, has raised $108 million in a Series B round co-led by Forerunner Ventures and Menlo Ventures, pushing its valuation past $1 billion - so-called unicorn status - just four months after its public launch.

Founded by former Plaid chief technology officer Jean-Denis Greze, Town gives each employee an AI assistant, called a Townie, that connects to tools such as email and calendars to help with everyday tasks. Townies can communicate with one another to share approved information and can now also interact with customers outside the company. Town says it reached millions of dollars in annual recurring revenue within weeks of launching.

The round, which also included Andreessen Horowitz, Serena Ventures and First Round Capital, brings Town's total funding to at least $160 million since a $55 million Series A in June. The speed of its rise highlights how quickly investors are willing to back enterprise AI agent startups, even with limited public track records.

Sources: TechCrunch · entARABI · Mezha.net

Key concepts in this story
Series B
An early-to-mid-stage funding round, usually raised once a startup has shown its product works and is gaining customers, used to scale the business further.
Unicorn
A private startup valued at $1 billion or more - a term coined because such companies were once considered as rare as mythical creatures, though they're now fairly common in tech.
Annual recurring revenue (ARR)
The amount of predictable revenue a company expects to collect every year from subscriptions, often used to gauge how fast a software business is actually growing - for example, $3 million in ARR means the company is on track to collect about $3 million a year from existing subscribers.
AI agent (reminder)
Software that can autonomously carry out multi-step tasks on a user's behalf, such as checking a calendar and drafting an email, rather than simply answering a single question.
Why it matters for you

Fast unicorn rounds for enterprise AI agent startups show where venture money and hiring demand are flowing right now - useful to mention if asked in an interview which part of the AI market looks most investable.

Code corner

Spotting 'distressed' debt with basis points

Based on: CoreWeave's bonds hit 'distressed' territory, dragging down other AI stocks

CoreWeave's bonds became 'distressed' when their yield spread over government bonds passed 1,000 basis points. Here's a tiny script that checks any bond against that threshold.

treasury_yield = 4.5  # % yield on a safe government bond

corporate_bonds = {
    "CoreWeave 2029": 15.2,
    "Oracle 2031": 6.1,
    "SafeCo 2030": 5.0,
}

def spread_in_bps(bond_yield, treasury_yield):
    return (bond_yield - treasury_yield) * 100

for name, bond_yield in corporate_bonds.items():
    spread = spread_in_bps(bond_yield, treasury_yield)
    distressed = spread >= 1000
    print(f"{name}: spread = {spread:.0f} bps, distressed = {distressed}")
Line by line
  1. treasury_yield stores the 'safe' baseline yield as a percentage, used to measure how much extra risk investors are pricing into other bonds.
  2. corporate_bonds is a dictionary mapping each bond's name to its current yield - dictionaries are ideal here because each bond needs a label and a value.
  3. spread_in_bps() converts the yield difference from percentage points into basis points by multiplying by 100, since 1 percentage point equals 100 basis points.
  4. The for loop goes through each bond, calculates its spread, and checks whether it meets or exceeds the 1,000 basis-point threshold that defines 'distressed' debt.
  5. f-strings like f"{spread:.0f}" format the number neatly, rounding to zero decimal places for a cleaner printout.

Try it: Add a new bond called 'YourStartup 2032' with a yield of 20.0, then change treasury_yield to 4.0 and see how the spread and distressed flag change.
