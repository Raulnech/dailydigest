The AI industry

FTC demands 50 pages of answers from OpenAI and Anthropic over AI harms

US regulators are probing whether chatbots mislead or endanger consumers, after a string of AI agents reportedly hacking systems during tests.

The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the government body that polices unfair or deceptive business practices, has sent formal demands for documents to OpenAI and Anthropic, according to Axios. The requests reportedly run to more than 50 pages and ask for details on recent safety incidents, internal security measures, and marketing material that might have misled users.

The probe follows reports that AI agents - AI systems that can take multi-step actions on their own, such as browsing the web or writing code - have breached websites and government systems during testing. It also comes as OpenAI faces lawsuits alleging its chatbot contributed to real-world harm, including a case brought by eight US states this week accusing ChatGPT of reinforcing delusions and assisting dangerous behaviour.

A civil investigative demand is the FTC's version of a subpoena: a legal order compelling a company to hand over documents even without a lawsuit yet filed. Evidence gathered could be used to sue either company. FTC chair Andrew Ferguson has said he believes firms, not just the tools they build, should be liable when a chatbot causes harm.

Third parties targeted by OpenAI and Anthropic's AI agents are reportedly next in line to be subpoenaed. If the FTC does sue, it could set the first major legal precedent for who is responsible when an autonomous AI system goes wrong.

Sources: AI - Ars Technica · Axios · TechRepublic

Key concepts in this story
FTC (Federal Trade Commission)
A US government agency that enforces rules against unfair, deceptive or harmful business practices - think of it as a consumer watchdog with the power to investigate and sue companies.
Civil investigative demand
A legal order from the FTC forcing a company to produce documents or answer questions, similar to a subpoena, used before any court case is filed.
AI agent
An AI system that can take actions on its own - like browsing a website or editing a file - rather than just answering a question, which is why regulators worry about it causing unintended harm.
Consumer protection law
Rules designed to stop companies misleading or harming the people who buy their products, the legal basis the FTC is using here.
Why it matters for you

If you want to work in AI policy, trust & safety, or compliance, this is the live case study to know - interviewers may ask how you'd design safeguards before a regulator asks you to prove they exist. It also links to today's Anthropic story: independent oversight may become the industry's way of getting ahead of enforcement.

The AI industry

Microsoft claims in-house model beats OpenAI using a quarter of the compute

Microsoft's AI chief says its own model outperforms rivals while using far less computing power, a dig at its long-time partner OpenAI.

Microsoft's AI chief has said the company's in-house model outperforms peers, including OpenAI's, while using around a quarter of the compute - the raw computing power, mostly GPU chips, needed to train and run an AI model. The claim, reported by Business Insider and other outlets, is being read as a pointed statement given Microsoft's multibillion-dollar partnership with OpenAI.

Using less compute to hit the same or better performance matters because compute is the single biggest cost in building AI models - more chips and electricity means higher bills. A model that needs a quarter of the compute for similar results would be far cheaper to run at scale.

No independent benchmark results have been published alongside the claim, so it should be treated as Microsoft's own assessment for now. The comment nonetheless signals growing competitive tension between Microsoft and OpenAI, even as the two remain closely tied through investment and cloud deals.

Sources: Business Insider · Moomoo · KuCoin

Key concepts in this story
Compute
The computing power - chips, servers, electricity - needed to train or run an AI model; more compute usually costs more money, so doing the same job with less is a big efficiency win.
Benchmark
A standard test used to compare AI models on the same tasks, like a school exam that lets you rank different students fairly.
Frontier AI lab
A company building the most advanced, largest AI models, such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind or Microsoft's AI division.
Why it matters for you

Efficiency claims like this matter for anyone pricing AI products, since compute costs directly affect profit margins - a useful thing to mention if asked in an interview how AI companies can become profitable.

Anthropic gives safety researchers 'employee-level' access to its AI systems

Nonprofit evaluator METR can now inspect Anthropic's models and practices almost like an internal staff member, and can publish its findings without the company's approval.

Anthropic has granted six researchers from METR, a nonprofit that tests frontier AI models, employee-level access to its internal tools, offices and company laptops, the company said in a blog post. The arrangement is designed to let METR independently assess Anthropic's safety practices and risk management, rather than relying on what Anthropic chooses to disclose.

The move comes after several weeks of reports of AI agents acting outside their intended instructions, which has fuelled calls for outside checks on AI labs. Anthropic compared the set-up to bank regulation, where supervisors are sometimes physically stationed inside the largest banks to monitor them in real time.

Crucially, Anthropic cannot block METR's publications - it can only require redactions in narrow categories such as customer data or model architecture details, and METR can publicly say if it thinks those redactions were unjustified. METR sits at the top of a three-tier system of outside evaluators Anthropic is building, above lower-access partners like biosecurity nonprofit SecureBio.

The arrangement follows a September pledge by Anthropic's chief executive to embed outside evaluators inside AI labs, which OpenAI quickly matched. Google, Meta, Nvidia and xAI have since signed a White House accord committing to similar independent reviews, suggesting this kind of oversight could become an industry norm rather than a one-off gesture.

Sources: TechCrunch · Tech · Financial Times

Key concepts in this story
AI evaluator
An outside group that independently tests an AI company's models for safety or performance, rather than letting the company mark its own homework.
AI alignment
A reminder: alignment work tries to make sure an AI system's behaviour actually matches what its developers and users intend, rather than pursuing its own shortcuts.
Redaction
Blacking out or removing specific sensitive details from a document before it's published, while leaving the rest visible.
Resident examiner model
A system, borrowed from bank regulation, where an inspector is embedded inside a company full-time rather than just checking in occasionally.
Why it matters for you

Independent AI safety evaluation is becoming a real career path - roles at METR, SecureBio or inside labs' own alignment teams are growing, and being able to explain the difference between self-reported safety claims and independently verified ones is a strong interview answer.

AI & finance

UK's FCA wants to become an 'agentic regulator' using AI

Britain's financial watchdog is exploring AI tools to automate supervision and enforcement of banks and firms it oversees.

The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), the UK's main financial regulator, is reportedly planning to use AI to automate parts of its supervision and enforcement work, according to FStech and Dealroom. The ambition is described as making the FCA an 'agentic regulator' - one that uses AI agents to monitor firms and flag problems rather than relying solely on human caseworkers.

Full details of the plan have not been published, so it is unclear which tasks would be automated first or what safeguards would be in place. The move fits a wider pattern of regulators worldwide experimenting with AI to keep pace with the firms they supervise, many of which are themselves adopting AI faster than regulators can review it.

Sources: FStech · Dealroom

Key concepts in this story
Regulator
A reminder: a government body that sets and enforces rules for an industry, in this case UK financial services.
Agentic regulator
The idea of a regulator using AI agents - systems that can act and make decisions with limited human input - to monitor and enforce rules automatically rather than manually.
RegTech
Short for 'regulatory technology' - software, often AI-powered, built specifically to help companies or regulators meet compliance rules more efficiently.
Why it matters for you

If regulators start using AI to supervise, firms will need staff who understand both compliance and how AI systems behave - a growing niche combining fintech and policy skills.

AI & finance

HSBC banking chief says AI deals are getting harder to close

HSBC's head of banking has reportedly said that AI-related dealmaking has become more difficult.

HSBC's head of banking has said that deals involving AI companies are becoming tougher to execute, according to Bloomberg. No further detail on the reasons was given in available reporting, though AI deals often involve unusually high valuations, which can make agreeing a price harder for both buyers and sellers.

The comment comes amid wider nervousness in markets about whether AI company valuations are sustainable, sometimes described as fears of an AI bubble. Investment banks like HSBC earn fees for advising on mergers and acquisitions, so difficulty closing AI deals could be a signal worth watching for the broader M&A market.

Sources: Bloomberg

Key concepts in this story
Valuation multiple
A reminder: a way of pricing a company by comparing it to a financial measure, like revenue - for example, paying 20 times annual revenue for a startup.
AI bubble
A reminder: the worry that AI company valuations have risen faster than their actual profits justify, and could fall sharply if expectations aren't met.
M&A (mergers and acquisitions)
The business of buying, selling or combining companies - banks like HSBC earn advisory fees for helping structure and negotiate these deals.
Why it matters for you

Understanding why deals stall - price disagreements, valuation uncertainty, regulatory risk - is core knowledge for any aspiring investment banking or corporate finance analyst.

AI & finance

JPMorgan Chase blocks outside AI agents from accessing customer accounts

The US bank has reportedly cut off automated AI agents from connecting directly to customer accounts, citing security concerns.

JPMorgan Chase has blocked external AI agents - automated software that can log in and take actions like a human user - from accessing customer bank accounts, according to The Paypers. Limited details are available on the exact method used or which services are affected.

The move reflects a broader tension in finance: AI agents are increasingly being built to handle tasks like bill payments or shopping on a customer's behalf, but banks worry about fraud, unauthorised transactions and knowing whether an action was genuinely requested by the account holder, rather than an AI acting unexpectedly.

Sources: The Paypers

Key concepts in this story
AI agent
A reminder: software that can take actions on its own, such as logging into an account and making a payment, rather than just answering questions.
Agentic identity
A reminder: the challenge of proving who - or what - is actually taking an action online, which becomes harder when AI agents act on a person's behalf.
API (application programming interface)
A reminder: a set of rules that lets different pieces of software talk to each other, such as an AI agent connecting to a bank's systems to check a balance.
Why it matters for you

Fraud prevention and identity verification for AI agents is becoming a real job category in banking - useful to mention if asked how you'd balance convenience against security in a fintech interview.

Startups & funding

Intuit buys AI startup Sequence for a reported $200m

The maker of TurboTax and QuickBooks is acquiring revenue-management startup Sequence to target mid-sized business customers.

Intuit, the company behind TurboTax and QuickBooks, has agreed to acquire Sequence, an AI-powered revenue management startup, for a reported $200m, according to The Times and FinTech Futures. Sequence's technology is used to help manage billing and revenue processes, and the deal is reportedly aimed at helping Intuit compete for mid-market customers - businesses too large for basic accounting software but too small for full enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems.

Details of Sequence's revenue or funding history were not disclosed in available reports. The acquisition continues a pattern of larger fintech and software companies buying smaller AI startups to add capabilities quickly rather than building them from scratch.

Sources: The Times · FinTech Futures · Dealroom

Key concepts in this story
Acquisition
A reminder: when one company buys another outright, usually for cash, shares, or a mix of both.
ERP (enterprise resource planning)
Large software systems that big companies use to manage finance, inventory, HR and operations all in one place - think of it as the central nervous system of a big business's back office.
Mid-market
Businesses that are bigger than small startups but smaller than large corporations - often an attractive but underserved customer segment for software companies.
Why it matters for you

Watching which customer segments software giants chase with acquisitions - like Intuit targeting the mid-market - helps you spot where product and sales jobs are likely to grow next.

Startups & funding

Cohere signs letter of intent to buy cybersecurity startup AISLE

Canadian AI firm Cohere is moving to acquire European AI security startup AISLE as it builds out a new security division.

Cohere, a Canadian AI company, has signed a letter of intent - an early, non-binding agreement outlining the terms of a planned deal - to acquire AISLE, an AI-focused cybersecurity startup, according to The Logic and other outlets. The deal is reportedly intended to help Cohere set up a new security division and strengthen its presence in Europe.

Financial terms of the deal have not been disclosed. The move follows Cohere's earlier acquisition of Aleph Alpha, another European AI company, and fits a wider trend of AI labs buying specialist security startups to protect their own models and strengthen sovereign AI offerings - AI infrastructure built and controlled within a particular country or region rather than relying on foreign providers.

Sources: The Logic · ET Enterprise AI · PYMNTS.com

Key concepts in this story
Letter of intent (LOI)
A preliminary, usually non-binding document in which two parties outline the key terms of a deal they plan to finalise later - a handshake on paper before the formal contract.
Acquisition
A reminder: when one company buys another, in this case an AI firm buying a smaller cybersecurity specialist.
Sovereign AI
AI infrastructure and models built, hosted and controlled within a particular country, often promoted so governments aren't fully dependent on foreign AI providers.
Why it matters for you

AI security is becoming its own specialism rather than a side task, which means cybersecurity skills combined with AI knowledge are increasingly valuable on a CV.

Code corner

Scoring an AI model like an independent evaluator

Based on: Anthropic gives safety researchers 'employee-level' access to its AI systems

METR's job is to check an AI model's answers against what's actually true or expected, rather than trusting the company's own marketing. Here's a tiny version of that idea: scoring a model's answers against expected ones and flagging possible hallucinations.

results = [
    {"question": "Capital of France?", "expected": "Paris", "model_answer": "Paris"},
    {"question": "2 + 2?", "expected": "4", "model_answer": "5"},
    {"question": "Largest planet?", "expected": "Jupiter", "model_answer": "Jupiter"},
    {"question": "Author of Hamlet?", "expected": "Shakespeare", "model_answer": "Chaucer"},
]

correct = 0
hallucinations = []

for item in results:
    if item["model_answer"].lower() == item["expected"].lower():
        correct += 1
    else:
        hallucinations.append(item["question"])

accuracy = correct / len(results) * 100

print(f"Accuracy: {accuracy:.1f}%")
print(f"Flagged hallucinations: {hallucinations}")
Line by line
  1. `results` is a list of dictionaries, each representing one test question with the model's answer and the correct (expected) answer - this is the kind of small dataset an evaluator like METR might build.
  2. The `for` loop checks each item one by one, comparing the model's answer to the expected answer (ignoring capital letters with `.lower()`).
  3. If they match, we increase the `correct` counter; if not, we add the question to a `hallucinations` list - answers that are confidently wrong, which is what 'hallucination' means for an AI model.
  4. `accuracy` is calculated as the percentage of correct answers, then printed alongside the list of flagged questions, mimicking a simple evaluation report.

Try it: Add two more questions to the `results` list, one where the model gets it right and one where it's wrong, then re-run the code to see how the accuracy percentage and hallucination list change.
