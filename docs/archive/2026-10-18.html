The AI industry

DeepSeek Releases Faster, Leaner AI Model in Latest Update

The Chinese AI lab says its newest release trims computing costs while speeding up responses, intensifying competition with pricier Western rivals.

DeepSeek has pushed out an updated version of its large language model, which the company says runs faster and uses fewer computing resources than its predecessor, according to Bloomberg. Details beyond the headline claims are limited, but the update continues DeepSeek's pattern of releasing cheaper, efficient models that rattle assumptions about how much it costs to build cutting-edge AI.

DeepSeek shot to global attention last year when it released a model that reportedly matched the performance of leading American systems at a fraction of the training cost. Since then, it has kept iterating, putting pressure on both Chinese and Western labs to justify their own spending on expensive chips and data centres.

If the efficiency claims hold up, it could mean cheaper AI tools for consumers and businesses, but also raises further questions about whether the billions being poured into AI infrastructure by rivals like OpenAI and Google are being spent wisely.

Sources: Bloomberg.com

Key concepts in this story
Inference
Inference is the process of an already-trained AI model actually answering a question or generating text. A 'leaner' model needs less computing power per answer, which lowers running costs.
Benchmark
A benchmark is a standard test used to compare AI models, like a set of exam questions. Companies use benchmarks to back up claims that a new model is 'faster' or 'better' than the last one.
Why it matters for you

Cheaper, efficient models mean more startups can afford to build AI products without needing huge compute budgets, which is good news if you're hoping to break into AI product or engineering roles rather than infrastructure.

The AI industry

Nvidia's Huang Warns He Could Cut Chip Supply to 'Unsafe' AI Builders

The Nvidia chief executive reportedly suggested the company might refuse to sell chips to customers whose AI software is judged unsafe.

Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang has reportedly said the company could stop supplying its powerful AI chips to customers building software it considers unsafe, according to TokenPost. The comment positions Nvidia, the world's dominant maker of the graphics processing units that power most AI systems, as a potential enforcer of safety standards across the industry.

Nvidia's chips underpin nearly every major AI lab's training and running of large models, giving the company unusual leverage. Until now, that leverage has mostly been discussed in terms of export controls limiting sales to certain countries, rather than safety-based restrictions on individual companies.

Exactly how Nvidia would judge 'unsafe' AI software, and whether it would actually act on such threats, remains unclear from the limited reporting. But the remark signals growing anxiety at the top of the AI supply chain about reputational and regulatory risk if customers deploy poorly tested systems.

Sources: TOKENPOST

Key concepts in this story
AI alignment
AI alignment means making sure an AI system actually does what its creators intend, rather than behaving in harmful or unpredictable ways. Huang's comment implies Nvidia wants its chips used only by companies taking this seriously.
GPU (graphics processing unit)
A GPU is a specialised chip, originally built for video game graphics, that is very good at the parallel maths needed to train and run AI models. Nvidia makes most of the GPUs used by major AI labs.
Why it matters for you

If chipmakers start policing safety, it creates new compliance and red-teaming job opportunities inside AI companies that want to keep their hardware supply secure.

The AI industry

Microsoft Pulls Out of AI Data Centre Deal, UK Builder Left Exposed

A British data centre developer has reportedly lost a major contract with Microsoft, raising questions about the pace of the UK's AI infrastructure buildout.

Microsoft has reportedly cancelled an AI-related agreement with a UK data centre developer, according to the Telegraph, in a setback for a company positioning itself as a pioneer of British AI infrastructure. Few details of the deal's size or terms have been disclosed.

Data centres, the large facilities packed with servers and chips that train and run AI models, have become one of the most capital-intensive parts of the AI boom. Big tech firms like Microsoft have been signing enormous contracts with developers worldwide to secure enough computing capacity, often locking in power supply and construction years in advance.

A cancelled deal of this kind suggests Microsoft may be reassessing its capacity needs or costs, which could unsettle other developers betting on long-term AI infrastructure demand, particularly in markets like the UK trying to attract such investment.

Sources: The Telegraph

Key concepts in this story
AI infrastructure
AI infrastructure covers the physical building blocks needed to run AI, mainly chips, data centres, and electricity supply. It is extremely expensive to build, which is why long-term contracts with big tech firms matter so much to developers.
Capital expenditure (capex)
Capex is money a company spends upfront on long-lasting physical assets, like buildings or chips, rather than day-to-day running costs. Data centre deals involve huge capex commitments that can take years to pay back.
Why it matters for you

Cancelled infrastructure deals are an early warning sign worth tracking if you're assessing AI bubble risk, especially when thinking about careers or investments tied to data centre construction and energy supply.

AI & finance

Hong Kong Sets Out AI Rulebook for Banks as Agentic Tools Spread

Hong Kong's financial regulator has published guidance for banks deploying AI, as autonomous 'agentic' systems move deeper into everyday banking.

Hong Kong has unveiled a new framework setting expectations for how banks should use artificial intelligence, as agentic AI applications, systems that can take actions on their own rather than just answering questions, gain traction in the industry, according to Crowdfund Insider.

Regulators worldwide are grappling with how to supervise AI in finance, where mistakes or misuse can directly affect customers' money and market stability. Hong Kong's move follows a pattern of financial hubs trying to set clear rules before AI adoption outpaces oversight, rather than reacting after problems emerge.

The framework is likely to influence how banks in the region pilot AI tools for tasks like fraud detection, customer service, and trading, while giving compliance teams a clearer rulebook to work from as agentic systems take on more responsibility.

Sources: Crowdfund Insider

Key concepts in this story
AI agent
An AI agent is software that can carry out multi-step tasks on its own, such as checking a customer's account and then processing a refund, rather than just generating a text response. Banks are cautious because agents can take real actions with real consequences.
Regulator
A regulator is a government body that sets and enforces rules for an industry, in this case Hong Kong's financial authority setting AI rules for banks. Regulators aim to protect consumers and the stability of the financial system.
Why it matters for you

Financial regulators writing AI-specific rules is creating demand for people who understand both compliance and how AI systems actually work, a useful niche to mention in interviews for fintech or RegTech roles.

South Korea Summons Bank Chiefs Over AI-Driven Hacking Incidents

Lawmakers have called in five Korean bank chief executives to explain security breaches reportedly linked to AI-powered hacking.

South Korea's National Assembly has summoned the chief executives of five banks to answer questions about hacking incidents reportedly involving AI-driven attacks, according to ChosunBiz. Specific details of the attacks and the banks involved were not fully disclosed in available reporting.

The move highlights a growing fear among regulators that AI tools, which can automate and speed up tasks like writing malicious code or crafting convincing phishing messages, are making cyberattacks on financial institutions more frequent and harder to stop. Banks hold sensitive customer data and move vast sums of money, making them prime targets.

Lawmakers questioning bank leaders directly suggests pressure is building for stronger cybersecurity rules in Korea's financial sector, and the episode may prompt other countries' regulators to scrutinise how banks are defending against AI-enhanced threats.

Sources: Chosunbiz

Key concepts in this story
Insider threat
An insider threat is a security risk that comes from someone with legitimate access to a system, such as an employee, rather than an outside hacker. AI can make these harder to detect by automating realistic-looking malicious activity.
Systemic risk
Systemic risk is the danger that a problem at one bank spreads and threatens the whole financial system, much like one infected domino knocking down the rest. Regulators worry that AI-driven hacks could trigger this kind of chain reaction.
Why it matters for you

This is a reminder that cybersecurity skills combined with AI literacy are increasingly valuable in banking, and a good talking point if asked in an interview how AI creates new risks as well as efficiencies.

AI & finance

Franklin Templeton Chief Warns AI Debt Deals Are Getting Too Complex

Jenny Johnson has reportedly cautioned that financing for AI data centres is becoming so intricate that she prefers shorter-term investments instead.

Franklin Templeton chief executive Jenny Johnson has reportedly flagged concerns about the complexity of debt financing being used to fund AI infrastructure, according to Stocktwits, saying she favours shorter-term investments as a result. This adds a prominent voice from traditional asset management to a growing chorus of unease about how the AI buildout is being paid for.

Much of the money fuelling new data centres and chip purchases has come not from company profits but from borrowed money, often structured through complex financial arrangements that can be hard for outside investors to fully assess. Johnson's comments suggest that even seasoned investors are struggling to judge the risks embedded in these deals.

If more major investors share this caution, it could make it harder or more expensive for AI infrastructure firms to raise new debt, potentially slowing the pace of expansion and adding fuel to broader debates about whether current AI spending constitutes a bubble.

Sources: Stocktwits

Key concepts in this story
Debt financing
Debt financing means raising money by borrowing rather than selling shares in a company, with a promise to pay it back with interest. It's riskier when the borrower's future earnings, like an AI firm's, are uncertain.
AI bubble
An AI bubble is the fear that AI company valuations and investment have risen far beyond what current profits justify, similar to the dot-com bubble of the early 2000s. If confidence collapses, heavily indebted firms could be hit hardest.
Why it matters for you

Understanding how AI infrastructure is actually financed, through debt rather than just cash, is a sharp interview talking point for finance roles and helps you spot bubble warning signs before headlines catch up.

Startups & funding

Intel and Qualcomm Reportedly Vie for Stake in Jim Keller's Tenstorrent

Two major chipmakers are said to be courting the AI chip startup founded by veteran engineer Jim Keller.

Intel and Qualcomm are reportedly both pursuing a deal with Tenstorrent, the AI chip startup led by well-known semiconductor engineer Jim Keller, according to Startup Fortune. Details of what form any deal might take, whether investment, partnership, or acquisition, were not specified in available reporting.

Tenstorrent designs its own AI processors as an alternative to Nvidia's dominant chips, an area that has attracted intense interest as companies look for cheaper or more specialised ways to run AI workloads. Keller has previously worked on chip designs at Apple, Tesla, and AMD, giving the startup significant credibility in engineering circles.

Interest from two major established chipmakers suggests Tenstorrent's technology or talent is seen as valuable enough to fight over, and any resulting deal could reshape the competitive landscape for AI chips beyond Nvidia's current dominance.

Sources: Startup Fortune

Key concepts in this story
ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit)
An ASIC is a chip custom-built for one specific job, like running AI calculations efficiently, rather than being a general-purpose processor. Tenstorrent designs this kind of specialised AI chip.
Strategic investor
A strategic investor puts money into a company not just for financial return but because the deal benefits its own existing business, such as a chipmaker gaining access to new chip designs. This differs from a venture capital firm investing purely for profit.
Why it matters for you

Competition for chip startups like Tenstorrent shows there's still room beyond Nvidia in the AI hardware market, useful context if you're considering hardware or chip design career paths.

Startups & funding

Low-Profile Investor Behind Ramp and Cognition Draws Conflict Questions

Scrutiny is growing over a little-known investor with stakes in multiple fast-rising AI and fintech startups.

A low-profile investor with backing in both the fintech company Ramp and the AI coding startup Cognition has come under scrutiny over potential conflicts of interest, according to Crypto Briefing. Specific details of the investor's identity and the nature of the concerns were limited in available reporting.

Investors who hold stakes across multiple competing or closely related startups can create conflicts, for example by gaining insight into one company's strategy while sitting on the board of a rival, or by steering deals in ways that benefit their own portfolio rather than the startup itself. Such concerns have become more common as a smaller pool of investors fund an increasing share of high-profile AI startups.

The episode is a reminder that as AI funding concentrates among a handful of well-connected investors, questions about transparency and fairness in how deals are struck are likely to intensify.

Sources: Crypto Briefing

Key concepts in this story
Strategic investor
A strategic investor is one that invests partly to gain business advantage rather than purely financial return, which can create conflicts if they hold stakes in rival companies. See today's Tenstorrent story for another example.
Funding round
A funding round is a specific occasion when a startup raises money from investors in exchange for shares, often labelled by stage such as Series A or Series D. Concerns arise when the same investor appears across many rounds in related companies.
Why it matters for you

If you're eyeing a role at a fast-growing startup, it's worth researching who's actually funding it and whether those investors also back your potential employer's rivals or partners.

Code corner

Comparing Token Costs Between Two 'AI Models'

Based on: DeepSeek Releases Faster, Leaner AI Model in Latest Update

DeepSeek claims its new model is leaner and cheaper to run. Here's a tiny simulation showing how comparing token usage and pricing between two fake model responses works in practice, without needing a real API key.

import json

# Fake API responses standing in for two different models
old_model_response = json.dumps({"model": "old-model", "tokens_used": 1200, "price_per_1k_tokens": 0.03})
new_model_response = json.dumps({"model": "new-model", "tokens_used": 800, "price_per_1k_tokens": 0.015})

def calculate_cost(response_json):
    data = json.loads(response_json)
    tokens = data["tokens_used"]
    price = data["price_per_1k_tokens"]
    cost = (tokens / 1000) * price
    return data["model"], cost

old_name, old_cost = calculate_cost(old_model_response)
new_name, new_cost = calculate_cost(new_model_response)

print(f"{old_name}: ${old_cost:.4f} for this request")
print(f"{new_name}: ${new_cost:.4f} for this request")

savings_pct = ((old_cost - new_cost) / old_cost) * 100
print(f"The new model is {savings_pct:.1f}% cheaper for the same kind of request")
Line by line
  1. We use `json.dumps` to create two fake JSON strings, mimicking what a real AI API might return, including how many tokens were used and the price per 1,000 tokens.
  2. The `calculate_cost` function parses the JSON back into a Python dictionary with `json.loads`, then calculates cost by dividing tokens by 1,000 and multiplying by the price.
  3. We call the function on both fake responses to get each model's name and cost.
  4. Finally, we calculate the percentage savings of the new model versus the old one, showing numerically how a 'leaner' model can mean real cost savings.

Try it: Change the tokens_used and price_per_1k_tokens values to match a real pricing page from an AI provider, then calculate the cost of a 5,000-token request for both models.
